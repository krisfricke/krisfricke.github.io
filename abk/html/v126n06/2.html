December in beekeeping is always interesting because while

people in most other walks of life are perhaps looking forward

to some holiday time, beekeepers are looking forward to

working harder than ever. If you’re a sideliner or hobbyist it

may be just having some time to catch up on the hives during

the time off your main job, if you’re a commercial beekeeper

working full time on bees anyway, its just a continuation of

working hard because the season doesn’t take a break, but

you might have some rellies that expect you to make time

for them so its even more hectic than usual. Me, I’ve always

been a beekeeper and often feel like I’m the only one working

Christmas week as the other agricultural workers usually busy

in the fields around the hives do seem to manage to get the

time off and for a week it’s just me alone in the fields.

The big news of the moment in the industry is that finally

one of the oxalic based miticides is available. And just past

press time (the editor’s note is the last thing written) I’ve

seen news that Apimondia won’t have a honey contest

due to rampant fraud these last few years, which is rather

depressing news. But the AHBIC varroa heat map shows very

little advance of varroa this month so there’s that.

Another interesting news item is that stingless bee honey

is now legal to sell in Australia. No one had previously been

prosecuted for breaking this rule but its nice to have it sorted

and established. We also have a good article about the

special properties of stingless bee honey and in particular

“trehalulose” a special sugar found in it. From personal

experience stingless bee honey is very interesting, very tart,

and if I didn’t live in southern Victoria I’d love to experiment

with keeping stingless bees.

We have two different articles about using smokers, one

about lighting them and one (in letters) about extinguishing

them. While smokers might seem like a topic so basic it’s not

worth talking about (and I still wonder why it’s a whole unit in

the Beekeeping Cert 3), I think the article about lighting them

will likely include some ideas you might not have come across

before; and the article about extinguishing them addresses

some topics that are oft overlooked in basic beekeeping

guides (should you extinguish your smoker at the end of the

day or to transit to a new location, what about stopping at a

servo with a lit smoker in the back?).

Last month we had a news item about a Deformed Wing

Virus vaccine, this month we have an article by Australian

researcher Olivia Ducommun-Dit-Verron on efforts using the

same technique to make an AFB vaccine here. Spoiler alert

but next month we’ll have yet another article on this theme.

Jim Masucci writes in “A Novel Treatment to Combat

Varroa” about another innovative anti-varroa treatment

method, in this case using strands of DNA (technically dsRNA,

but, well, read the article for the details). People often ask

me, all the money going into research on varroa treatments,

what are they researching – well it’s all kinds of things from as

“simple” as breeding to things as complex as this.

And speaking of breeding, we have an infographic from

Agrifutures on “Breeding for Resistance,” an article by British

researcher Francis Ratnieks on breeding better crops for bees

(which is a slightly different take than we usually think about,

but something we’d certainly welcome), and a follow up

article to last month’s on Indiana Mite Biters about people

working to spread those genetics. This latter article I think is

more significant than merely some stories about some things

some people did in the States, this is about a number of people

working together to create some large collaborations to

spread good varroa resistant genetics. Created at a university

breeding program these genetics were (and continue to be)

distributed through coordinated efforts by not only large

club networks but clubs working collaboratively with other

clubs, and as well large commercial beekeepers are involved

so I hope a variety of people will read that and start thinking

what collaborations they can start here to ensure maximum

effectiveness of our own breeding programs.

Rounding things out we have another set of DIY instructions

from Ed Simon – a DIY box jig, which is a must-have if you’re

going to assemble more than a handful of hive bodies; and

Randy Oliver’s continuing research on anti-robbing screens.

Normally research is seldom considered worth writing about

unless it finds a positive result, but Randy’s lack of a positive

result in his anti-robbing screen research is truly interesting

and has definitely changed the way I think and talk about

anti-robbing screens. He’s continuing this research, including

trying some things to see if he can make them more effective,

and I for one am very interested to see what he finds.

Altogether I’m optimistic we’ve once again put together a

good mix of interesting material to keep you abreast of the

latest in beekeeping conversations and provide some reading

material during whatever brief breaks you can find between

beekeeping and social duties during the holidays!

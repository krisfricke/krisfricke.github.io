Despite this advancement in understanding honey bee

immunity, the effectiveness of TGIP in honey bees remains

controversial. Studies on TGIP’s role against bacterial

and viral pathogens have shown inconsistent outcomes,

suggesting that factors such as exposure route, host

genetics or epigenetics, and pathogen type may influence

its effectiveness. For instance, TGIP has demonstrated

some protective effects against P. larvae infection, but

studies involving Melissococcus plutonius, the causative

agent of European Foulbrood (EFB), have shown no

protective benefits.3,14,15 Most research on TGIP in honey

bees has used heat-killed bacteria as the pathogen source,

and the lack of response to certain pathogens may be

due to heat-induced damage to pathogen fragments that

would otherwise trigger an immune reaction.15

In 2014, a groundbreaking study by López et al., (2014)6

demonstrated that honey bee queens exposed to heat-

killed P. larvae bacteria could transfer increased immunity

to their offspring. This research found that the offspring

of these pathogen-exposed queens were 26% more

likely to survive when faced with infectious challenge.

However, injecting queens with pathogens posed practical

challenges, including potential harm to the queens due

to injury and stress.6 The underlying mechanism for this

immune transfer in honey bees remained unknown until

2015, when Salmela et al., (2015)12 identified Vg, an egg

yolk protein, as the carrier of immune-priming signals

from the queen to her offspring (Figure 2). This discovery

exoskeleton cuticle and the peritrophic membrane

Secondary defence mechanisms involve both cellular and

humoral responses, which provide the machinery required

for vaccine efficacy.7,10 Examples of cellular/humoral

mechanisms include lymphatic structures and chemical

signalling systems, such as cytokines and antibodies in

humans. In honey bees, this function is carried out by

fat bodies, which synthesise vitellogenin (Vg). However,

unlike humans, honey bees lack an adaptative immune

system, and therefore do not produce antibodies, which

are the foundations for many vaccine developments.8,11

Although honey bees lack antibodies, they do produce

immune effectors that generate a functional immune

response following pathogen exposure.8,11. Furthermore,

maternal immunity has been observed, wherein an immune

response can be passed down to offspring, positively

influencing their pathogen resistance and survival, known

as trans-generational immune priming (TGIP).3,6, This

discovery in honey bees significantly advanced the world

of research with a potential vaccine option, with the use

of TGIP to prime the honey bee immune response upon

exposure to pathogen fragments such as peptide/proteins,

lipids and nucleic acid via injection or oral delivery.6

TGIP mechanisms are still not fully understood, however

it is proposed that TGIP operates along two pathways:

the nurse hypopharyngeal axis and the queen ovary axis

(Figure 2). Vitellogenin (Vg) acts as a transporter, carrying

pathogen fragments to the queen’s ovaries and the nurse

bees’ hypopharyngeal glands. In these glands, royal jelly is

produced and serves as a means to transfer the pathogen

fragments to both the queen and young larvae.12,13 Once

these fragments reach their target tissue (the fat bodies,

hemocytes and epithelial tissue), they trigger an enhanced

immune response by binding to unidentified receptors,

initiating signalling pathways that activate immune-

related genes. Notably, the upregulation of the Vg and

defensin-1 genes is essential in promoting immunity in

honey bees via TGIP.13

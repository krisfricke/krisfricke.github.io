unprecedented challenges . While commercial operations

grapple with large-scale impacts, hobbyist beekeepers face

their own crisis. Experience from other countries suggests

we could see up to a 50% reduction in hobbyist and semi-

commercial operations, threatening both local honey

availability and the crucial pollination services these beekeepers

provide to suburban gardens and small-scale farms.

These bees play a vital role in pollinating 35 agricultural

crops across Australia, including nuts, berries, and stone

fruits . The anticipated decimation of feral bee populations

poses a particular threat to both commercial crops and native

ecosystems. With feral bee populations expected to decline

by up to 95%, the loss of these free pollination services will

force greater reliance on managed hives, further driving up

production costs that will inevitably be passed on to consumers.

In September 2023, after an intensive eradication

campaign that cost $132 million and saw the destruction of

approximately 30,000 hives, Australian authorities made the

difficult but necessary decision that eradication was no longer

technically feasible . The current national management plan,

endorsed by the federal government’s National Management

Group, has capped response costs at $100 million. This plan

includes the rollout of 100 full-day workshops for beekeepers

across the country and the deployment of 32 extension

officers to support the education campaign - crucial steps in

helping both commercial and hobbyist beekeepers adapt to

this new reality .

The scientific community recognizes that this transition

period also presents unique research opportunities. Professor

Sasha Mikheyev from the Australian National University

emphasizes the urgent need to gather baseline data about

Australian bees and ecosystems before Varroa’s full impact

is felt. This research window, while rapidly closing, offers

a chance to document and understand the changes our

ecosystem will undergo as Varroa becomes established.

Under the new management paradigm, beekeepers

must report any suspected Varroa mite detections to apiary

officers through the Exotic Plant Pest Hotline, as mandated

by the Biosecurity Act 2015. The industry has standardized

three primary testing methods - sugar shake testing, alcohol

wash monitoring, and drone uncapping inspections - with

successful operations typically employing a combination of all

three for comprehensive monitoring.

For consumers, the impact of these changes will become

increasingly apparent at the checkout. Common breakfast

items like almonds could see price increases of up to 50%.

Fresh produce such as apples, pears, cucumbers, and zucchini

may become more expensive as pollination costs rise. Even

everyday items like canola oil, used in many processed foods,

could see price increases as production costs rise due to

increased pollination expenses.

Australia’s position as one of the last major beekeeping

nations to face Varroa offers both challenges and opportunities.

While we can learn from international experiences, our unique

climate and agricultural systems require tailored solutions.

Strategies effective in Tasmania’s cool climate may require

significant modification for Queensland’s tropical conditions.

This variation drives the development of region-specific

approaches to control and treatment.

The path forward requires a comprehensive approach that

considers not just beekeepers and their bees, but the entire

ecosystem they support. Government support through

research funding, compensation schemes, and biosecurity

measures plays a crucial role in industry adaptation.

However, amidst these challenges, there are reasons for

optimism. Australia’s late exposure to Varroa means we can

learn from the successes and failures of other countries.

Our strong biosecurity systems and world-class research

institutions are already developing innovative solutions tailored

to Australian conditions. The beekeeping community, both

commercial and hobbyist, has shown remarkable resilience

and adaptability in the face of this challenge.

Moreover, the collaborative spirit within the Australian

beekeeping community offers hope. Knowledge sharing

between commercial and hobbyist beekeepers, supported by

the national workshops and extension officers, will be crucial

in developing effective management strategies. The industry’s

ability to adapt and innovate, demonstrated throughout

its history, suggests that while Varroa presents a significant

challenge, it need not spell the end of Australian beekeeping.

While the future of Australian beekeeping will look

different, the industry’s commitment to adaptation and

innovation provides a path forward. Through continued

collaboration, research, and support, both commercial and

hobbyist beekeepers can develop sustainable management

practices that allow them to thrive despite Varroa’s presence.

The journey ahead may be challenging, but the Australian

beekeeping community’s resilience, combined with our

advantages in facing this threat, offers hope for a sustainable

future for our pollinators and the vital role they play in our

food security and ecosystem health.

Smoker Extinguishing

I have beehives at several locations,

sometimes over an hour apart, should

I keep my smoker lit while traveling

between apiaries?

*Dave

Hi Dave,

This is indeed a question frequently

not covered in beginning beekeeping

guides and not considered until one

suddenly finds oneself faced with the

situation. I myself at first felt guilty for

not thoroughly extinguishing my smoker

before traveling, it wasn’t until I asked

several fellow beekeepers what they did

and found they all also don’t extinguish

their smoker to travel between apiaries

that I felt confident this wasn’t a guilty

misbehavior but best practice.

First let’s address the reasons not to

extinguish it in the field. There are two

methods to extinguish a smoker, one is

to dump it out and bury the contents, the

second is to pour water in the smoker.

Because we’re often working around dry

grass that is highly flammable, dumping

it out is frequently not a safe option,

and one wouldn’t want to get into the

habit or dependence on doing so when

it’s safe only to later find oneself in an

unsafe situation without options.

The second option is to pour water

into the smoker to extinguish it, but this

is obviously not a suitable method either

if you plan to use the smoker later that

day as even if you replace all the fuel a

wet smoker still doesn’t work very well.

But one doesn’t want to just drive

down the road with the smoker in the

back of the ute. From past experience

(yes I’ve made all the mistakes at some

point, this is how you learn what not to

do), the onrush of air past even a smoker

you thought had gone out of its own will

reignite it to be billowing like a steam

train – this results in a danger of sparks

flying out into the verge of the road,

and/or at the very least other motorists

desperately honking their horn at you as

they think your ute is on fire.

Blocking the opening of the smoker

is enough to prevent this though. I’ve

seen corks or bits of wood used for this

purpose, or used a bit of duct tape (I

always have duct tape on the ute, I’m a

firm believer in its numerous uses for all

kinds of situations, including once in an

old ute the battery came loose and I had

to use duct tape to secure it in position

but I digress). Another popular option

is old ammunition boxes from army

surplus stores, though I’ve heard some

reports that they can be air tight enough

they’ll extinguish your smoker entirely.

Another alternative is to buy a specially

made smoker box such as some retailers

sell (google “beekeeping smoker box”

or else you’ll get a bunch of barbecue

related equipment), in which you can

easily put the smoker and also not

worry about it rolling around or igniting

something nearby by contact.

On that note, always make sure your

smoker is well away from anything

flammable such as spare smoker fuel or

metho for alc washes or tool sanitizing.

Another particular circumstance to be

avoided is what if you suddenly realize

you need to stop for fuel, but the smoker

is lit in the back? While I haven’t heard

any story of this being a problem or read

anything else about it, I personally fear a

dangerous outcome from a smoker with

burning temperatures inside it and petrol

vapors at the service station. For this

reason I always try to hit the servo before

I’ve lit the smoker for the day. When I’ve

been caught out absolutely in need of a

fuel stop I’ve actually stopped just inside

the servo entrance, put the smokerbox

containing the smoker on the kerb and

retrieved it on my way out again (don’t

forget it!)

Even at the end of the day I (and other

beekeepers I’ve asked) don’t extinguish

the smoker, for essentially the same

reasons – if you put it under the faucet

to extinguish it it will still be thoroughly

damp if you want to use it the next day

and even at your own workshop it’s safer

to let it burn itself out inside itself than

dumping that out to some secondary

location. Plus if you’re a professional

beekeeper you’ll never know when

sudden

inspiration

or

unforeseen

circumstances will spur you to do more

beekeeping even after you’ve thought

you finished for the day. Similar to

stowing the smoker in the ute, plug the

opening or leave it in a secure container

and make sure nothing flammable is

next to it. The main downside to letting

it burn out is the build up of creosote in

it, especially from pine needles, which

necessitates periodic cleaning, but that’s

a topic for another day.

For more on actually lighting a smoker,

see “A Smoker Lighting Trick” on page 48.

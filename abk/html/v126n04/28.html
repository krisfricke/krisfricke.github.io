Drone-laying queens vs. laying workers

I have a wonderful friend and mentor from the very

beginnings of my beekeeping days named Ken Kloepper.

He raises Varroa Sensitive Hygiene (VSH) Cordovan queens

and nucs for sale, so he needs moving permits every year.

We’ll spend a day visiting his tidy, precisely plumb apiaries.

We love to engage in counterpoint discussions on several

beekeeping subjects. One favorite topic is whether drone-

laying queens or laying workers dominate particular queen-

wrong colonies. This discussion involves much more than

semantics. In the grand scheme of things, it may not make

much of a difference whether or not unfertilized eggs are

being laid by an unviable queen or by workers. Both result

in drones. Both are frustrating problems needing correction,

and lacking easy methods of doing so.

With egg laying, there are a few markers exclusive to

drone-laying queens and other markers associated with

laying workers. Morphometrically, the eggs themselves don’t

look much different to the naked eye. A 1994 study by J.

Woyke published in Apidologie measured and compared

the size of laying worker eggs versus queen eggs and found

that eggs laid by a queen tended to be about 1.5 mm long

and 0.4 mm wide, while laying workers laid diverse sizes of

eggs — sometimes smaller, sometimes larger, and any sizes

in between.

To better identify which imposter is laying unfertilized

eggs, look at egg position and number of eggs in a cell.

While a properly mated queen almost always stands her

egg straight up in the center of a cell, some drone-laying

queens may lay eggs low on the cell wall, because they

may have shorter abdomens and can’t reach the bottom of

the cell. Drone-laying queens usually lay only one egg per

cell. Laying workers, on the other hand, might lay multiple

eggs per cell. Eggs might be stacked up as laying workers

indiscriminately drop them into any cell they can find,

empty or already full of eggs. They will also lay eggs on

pollen, which queens seldom do.

Popcorn brood and eggs on pollen are excellent signs

that a beekeeper needs to step in and requeen a hive. But

timing is everything. Beekeepers trying to “cut the problem

off at the pass” by arbitrarily requeening three weeks after

swarm season can do more harm than good. I have seen

a number of beekeepers take this approach, resulting in

the death or absence of both the purchased queen and the

newly appointed postswarm queen as they either fight to

the death of both or swarm away, leaving hives hopelessly

queenless. It’s better to play the waiting game after swarm

season by inspecting regularly and staying patient. Colonies

don’t develop laying workers overnight. Endure a month of

post-swarm queenlessness before ordering a new queen.

When feeding a row of colonies in buildup, take note of “slow

feeders.” This is often a sign of weakness and a signal for a

beekeeper to step in.

I was inspecting nucs my friend Big T had made a few

weeks earlier. Most of his nucs were strong and had rapid

traffic coming and going from their entrances. But a few

entrances were quiet. Peering under the covers of these

quiet hives, we noticed their Mason jars of sugar syrup were

still full and starting to grow a little mold. Getting under

the hood and into the brood nest, we found rows of puffy

capped cells, sometimes poorly patterned. Drone-laying

queens. I felt Big T’s pain, because I myself, like so many

other beekeepers, experience this same problem annually.

It’s always a hassle, righting a brood nest full of drones.

In best-case scenarios, drone-laying colonies identified

early enough (within a week or two of queenlessness) can

sometimes be saved just by adding a queen cell or two. Queen

cells can be carefully stolen from late-swarming hives, or

from colonies that recently lost their queen and can still draw

queen cells from a frame of just-hatched eggs. Early in the

process, a new drone-laying queen just getting started might

not have the full allegiance of the colony, so the addition of

available queen cells can be a powerful weapon. Another

early-stage option is to combine the drone-laying colony with

a queenright hive, using a double screen temporarily to keep

the colonies separate until the entire colony is accustomed to

the pheromone of the viable queen.

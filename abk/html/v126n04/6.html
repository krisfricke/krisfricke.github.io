Rhubarb Pie

I’ve heard rhubarb leaves are the secret

silver bullet to treating Varroa without

chemicals, is that true?

Barbara

75mg of oxalic acid per leaf1. It does not offgas at all from the leaves (it takes a very

hot sublimator to render it to vapor form)2, so quite frankly the mites, who are not

at all vegetarian, are never going to come into contact with it. And even if they did,

that amount is certainly nothing near anything considered an efficacious treatment

amount anywhere. In fact, for context, that amount in total is approximate to what

the American USDA considers “negligible residues” per kilogram of honey – “the

amount of OA present in honey as a residue after application for varroa treatment

in hives is negligible, approximately 60-80 mg/kg.”3 If the total amount per leaf is

considered negligible even if multiplied by the number of kilos of honey in a hive, it is

safe to say it is an irrelevantly small amount.

Another way to answer this though, I think, is a sort of “the proof is in the pudding”

approach, or in this case, the rhubarb pie – if it worked well, we’d see wide uptake

in the places where varroa has been for awhile, after all we’re not the only ones with

the internet.

A 2023 survey of UK beekeeper’s varroa treatment habits titled “A survey of UK

beekeeper’s Varroa treatment habits”4 listed rhubarb leaves as 0.3% of treatments.

I searched published papers for any information about rhubarb leaf use for varroa

control and the sole result I could find was in the 2023 paper “Varroa Control: A Brief

Overview of Available Methods” published in Bee World.5 Under the subheading of

“Non-proven methods” it says among other things

“...rhubarb leaves are examples of varroa treatments for which there is

no proof of their efficacy. For example, placing a rhubarb leaf on top of

the hives will result in a higher mite fall because of the bees’ activity to

remove the leaf. The same inadequate effect can also be achieved with

toilet paper, kitchen towel paper, etc.”

Additionally, I asked pre-eminent varroa authority Dr Jamie Ellis6 about rhubarb leaves.

After first noting the paucity of research, and that it means not only do we not know how

it affects mites, we equally don’t know how rhubarb might affect bees, he continues:

Second, oxalic acid has a hard time controlling Varroa when used as

a commercially available product. I highly doubt that rhubarb has any

impact at all. Why?...

Third, because rhubarb has such low concentrations of OA in it. Like I

said, ‘high’ doses of pure OA often don’t work well, especially if brood is

present in the hive.

In the science world, I try ‘never to say never,’ but it is highly unlikely

that rhubarb has any impact on Varroa.

So I think that pretty much covers it. In summary, oxalic is present in rhubarb leaves

in smaller amounts than what the USDA calls a negligible residue of a treatment, it is

not “not a chemical” just because it’s coming from a leaf, the mites will never come in

contact with it in that medium, and it has no more proven efficacy than a wet paper

towel. So I would say, don’t be a rube, Barbara, there’s plenty of organic (Apiguard

(oil of Thyme) and formic acid) and even efficacious genuinely chemical free (drone

cell culling, screened bottom boards, breeding for resistance) treatment options. Your

best use of that rhubarb you’ve now bought is to make a nice rhubarb pie, or perhaps

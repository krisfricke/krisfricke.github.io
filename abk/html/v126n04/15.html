Conclusion:

Saturday is a popular day for stealing honey, as are Mondays,

Wednesdays, and Thursdays (the outlier on Thursday, indicated

by the open circle, likely groups it with the other high theft

days). Sundays saw the lowest rate of honey theft. Perhaps

would-be honey thieves are slightly religious?

Question:

Do we get robbed more at certain times of day?

Rationale:

The early bird gets the worm, but the second mouse gets

the cheese – do honey thieves have a preferred time of day?

Data:

Conclusion:

People who steal honey are just as likely to strike during

the week as on the weekend.

Question:

Does the day of the week matter?

Rationale:

Thieves don’t seem to distinguish between weekdays

versus weekends, but maybe there’s a day of the week that’s

preferred? Mondays, we watch football; Wednesdays, we

steal honey.

Data:

Conclusion:

Honey is stolen throughout the day. Honey put out in the

afternoon had a 15% chance of being stolen, compared with

7% in the morning, but this difference was not statistically

significant. Therefore, honey thieves do not appear to have

preferred times to be active.

Question:

Does it matter how long we’ve had the honey stand up for?

Rationale:

We don’t have a honey stand up all year, so maybe honey

thieves get especially excited when the stand first goes up.

Or maybe when they’ve seen the honey stand up for a while,

they think we’re drowning in honey, so they might as well

help us out by stealing some.

Data:

Figure 2: The proportion of honey stolen by days of the

week. Letters above boxplots denote statistical groups. Honey

put out on Saturdays had the highest probability of being

stolen (an average of 31% was stolen or partially paid for).

Figure 4: The proportion of honey that was stolen from the

stand each day, from 19 July to 20 September 2016. The blue

line shows the statistically significant negative relationship

between theft and date. The gaps (day 18-33 and 44-52) show

dates when the honey stand was being repaired, and honey

was not for sale (see below).

Figure 3: Honey theft by time of day. Honey put out for sale

in the morning (6am – 10am), midday (11am – 3pm), or

afternoon (4pm – 8pm) had the same chance of being stolen.

Conclusion:

Date had a significant effect on theft rates, with more

honey being stolen when the honey stand was first set

up. Honey put out in the first 10 days had an 18% chance

of being stolen, compared with 8% in the last 10 days.

Consumers didn’t know when we were going to take down

the honey stand, so there wasn’t a risk of thieves rushing

to steal one last jar before the end of the selling/stealing

season. That thieves were more likely to strike earlier in the

season might be because once they had stolen one jar, they

were satiated, and didn’t need to steal additional jars. For

future experiments, we could see if/when theft rates would

increase again. Perhaps it overlaps with the amount of time it

takes a honey thief to consume a jar of honey? (“Dear, we’re

running low on honey, would you mind stealing another jar

from that quaint honey stand run by the graduate students

down on Freese Road?”).

I mean, hey, it’s grad school, and $10 is $10. Might one

of us be pocketing funds and then saying it’s been stolen?

Sure, the PI is a respectable scientist, but is he a good human

being?

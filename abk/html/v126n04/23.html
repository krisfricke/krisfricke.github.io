Benefits of the New Approach

This method allows the beekeeper to keep the queen out

of the honey super while the frames are being drawn out

and gives all the worker bees full, unrestricted access into

the honey super.

An additional benefit to this approach could be to place a

green, drone comb frame into the isolation cage, preferably

already drawn out, and allow the queen to lay drones on the

drone comb. The beekeeper could then remove the green,

drone comb and once it is mostly capped over, remove it

and freeze it to kill all the mites that have been attracted

onto the green drone comb. Much work is being done on

using adult drones to sample for mites, and this might be

a useful method, allowing the drones to emerge within

the isolation cage, as they cannot squeeze their way out,

and they can be used for mite sampling at key times of the

beekeeping season.

Conclusion and Recommendations

If you do not have a queen frame isolation cage, I strongly

recommend you obtain one from [most beekeeping

equipment retailers] so that you can take advantage of

its many uses, especially in helping get your honey supers

drawn out quickly in the Spring.

I needed to add an undrawn honey super onto a two-deep

hive. But the problem was that my queen was laying like

crazy. I didn’t feel I could keep her out of my super without

using a queen excluder, but I didn’t want to restrict the flow

of workers into the super. I use full-frame queen isolation

cages in raising queens and for testing mites. If you’ve never

seen one, they are made from metal queen excluder material

and shaped larger than a frame so that a deep or medium

frame can be placed in this cage with a lid. It isolates the

queen on a full deep or medium frame.

Implementing the New Technique

I placed a deep frame of mostly drawn comb with open

cells into the isolation cage and moved the queen into the

cage. I decided to hold her in this cage for six days. Since a

deep frame has almost 7,000 cells, it would take about a

week for the queen to run out of room. Meanwhile, I could

place my undrawn honey super on the hive, and since we

were experiencing a heavy nectar flow, I knew they would

draw out the comb on my honey frames.

In six or seven days, I went back to observe what had

happened. It worked! My honey super frames were 75%

drawn out with the start of nectar being added, preventing

the queen from laying in the honey super if she were to

wander up there. My queen was happy hanging out in her

isolation cage, so I released her and removed the cage. I

documented this experiment in a recent YouTube video, and

I’ll leave info at the bottom of this article so you can observe

this work more closely.

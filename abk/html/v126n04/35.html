The Varroa Controller the hyperthermia device from Austria

Most beekeepers with whom I speak are reluctant to use chemical treatments to control

varroa and in my opinion, with good reason. The synthetic chemical options are lipophilic

and very stable. Their chemical attraction to fats (lipids) results in a build-up in beeswax

and the chemical stability means it doesn’t break down in any reasonable time frame.

If you use the treatments according to the label, the

chemicals should not exceed the maximum residual level

or MRL.

All chemical treatments take a toll on the bees, even

the non-synthetic or organic chemicals. I’ve seen piles of

dead bees, larvae and pupae near the hive entrance with

every form of registered or permitted chemical. Heavily

parasitised bees are most prone to succumb to the effects

of the chemicals. It’s a balance between killing mites and

minimising the harm to the bees.

Hyperthermia – A Viable Alternative?

Using heat to control varroa has been researched

since the 1970s with valuable data collected and some

disappointing results in field trials. We know that 42°C

renders varroa unviable, either killing them or disrupting

their reproduction. At 44°C bee larvae and pupae start to

die or become unviable. It’s also not a good idea to expose

your queen to these temperatures. It takes a heavy toll on

her store of sperm. It’s a fine line that has been the main

challenge in using heat against varroa.

Attempts to heat whole hives or brood chambers to the

critical 42°C are thwarted by the bees’ very efficient ability

to control the internal environment of the hive. Their

built-in air-conditioning team go into overdrive to cool the

temperature back to the preferred level of about 34°C.

Austrian scientist Dr. Wolfgang Wimmer applied the

research in a different way. By relocating brood frames

without the nurse bees to a separate chamber, he had

total control of the temperature. After a series of trials to

prove the strategy and perfect the technique, he released

the Varroa Controller onto the market in 2011. Since

that time, European commercial and hobby beekeepers

have successfully managed varroa without any chemical

intervention using the Varroa Controller. The original

devices are still in use attesting to the quality of build.

How does the Varroa Controller Work?

The device looks a little like a large yellow, well insulated

Esky. But there are some all-important differences

between a cooler and the Varroa Controller.

Temperature and humidity are critical, so the heater

unit is very stable and the electronics controlling the

temperature is state of the art with a precision temperature

probe that is inserted into the middle frame. Air is gently

circulated to maintain an even temperature throughout

the chamber and demineralised water is used to maintain

the correct humidity.

There are no settings or adjustments to be done,

removing any potential user error. The precision electronics

keeps the brood frames at 41.5°C for two hours. The

cycle finishes with an alarm to let the apiarist know it’s

time to return the frames to the hives.

What is the Process?

The standard model accommodates 20 frames of capped

brood. With some careful management, it’s possible to

treat ten hives at a time. By restricting the queen in a

two-frame isolation cage called the Duplex Framebox, all

the foundress mites in the hive are drawn to those brood

frames like moths to a flame. This effectively traps 90%

of the mites in two frames of capped brood. An extra

benefit is that of effective swarm control due to the of

reduced bee population.

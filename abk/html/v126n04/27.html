There is never a guarantee that we will find a queen in

the hive, yet a keen eye for the queen is paramount in the

weeks before and after swarm season. Finding her before

she swarms and caging her, clipping her wings, or making

a split can stop a hive from swarming, reserving the full

working population for colony increase, wax building and

honey harvest. Having an eye for the queen means keeping

your eyes on the brood. At any given time, a beekeeper

should know their queen status, and this is never more true

than right after swarm season.

But the weather can slow this process. A young queen can

wait inside a gloomy hive for days on end, while worker bees

and beekeepers grow impatient. Beekeepers who understand

that, from emerging, a queen can take anywhere from 10

days to a month before she will have visible eggs in the cells,

can stay busy with other seasonal concerns, checking back

as needed, without alarm.

There is no rushing the coronation of a honey bee queen.

She has to do more than fly around, playing the field. She

has to be properly mated. An adequate number of fuzzy

fellas have to “bring her flowers” to result in a spermatheca

population from 3-7 million spermatozoa. It’s easy as time

goes on to fall into the trap of thinking a hive is queenless

when, in fact, the queen is still “drifting.” Beekeepers

have to resist buying a queen two and three weeks after

the swarm event. Returning weekly to a hive governed by

a virgin queen is unnerving, watching frame after frame of

comb become honeybound, but it’s important to keep your

cool. Check back weekly even up to one month after the

initial swarm before interfering with natural succession.

Hear ye, hear ye! Here she is!

Behavior is a helpful tipoff to spotting your newly matured

queen on the frame. The queen is the only bee in the hive

with fully developed ovaries. Once she reaches maturity, she

will produce queen mandibular pheromone (QMP). This is

a powerful chemical that suppresses ovary development

of workers, influences higher levels of vitellogenin and

dopamine levels, and inhibits juvenile hormone production,

thereby keeping nurses in the hive to care for brood for a

longer time. QMP is the “pheromone bouquet” that draws

a retinue of attendants around a queen as they collect it and

pass it from worker to worker. Use nurse bee behavior as

a guide when looking for a new laying queen. Nurses are

deeply engaged in their work under her rule and expression

of QMP, but you may notice them turn to face her, encircling

her where she slowly crawls across the frame with her long

spider-like legs.

Worker behavior can be more erratic without a queen or

when a queen is less than viable. Colonies often buzz in unison

with a sound that resembles roaring or crying. In the absence

of QMP, the ovaries of some workers become activated and

workers have an option to stay in the hive and lay eggs rather

than forage. Laying worker bees, incapable of taking mating

flights, and possessing underdeveloped ovaries, can only lay

unfertilized eggs, and therefore only produce drones. For such

a colony, the inability to produce workers to forage and raise

brood is a death sentence to the colony, served quickly. This is

why post-swarm inspections can’t be put off and beekeepers

need to inspect beyond just the supers.

Queen status inspections are an opportunity to get deep

inside a brood nest. There are two particular inspections to

schedule after the last swarm has flown. One inspection

confirms that the new queen returned from mating voyages

and has started laying. The second is a brood nest inspection

to ensure that the queen is actually laying a healthy pattern

of fertilized eggs —worker brood.

Queen status — Is she worthy of her crown?

Hers is a fairytale told backward. The story begins with

the climax. A hive swarms and a growing season begins.

When landscapes burst with flowers from the tops of trees

down to the soil, bees become possessed with the swarm

impulse. Colonies trick their queens into laying in the traps

we know as “queen cups.” From there, worker bees draw

queen cells and begin to uproot their queen, putting her on

a diet and stashing nectar often randomly in the brood nest,

interrupting her focused laying pattern. Often before queen

cells are capped, the queen takes the hint, and takes to the

sky, and takes half of her subjects, her loyal workers. Her

climactic exit begins the story of the new season.

Is it lonely for the young nurse bees left behind by the

swarm who watched their big sisters clear the halls?

Workers returning from distant foraging expeditions who

missed the exodus — do they wonder where everybody

went? Some colonies seem to suffer a “fear of missing

out,” issuing “afterswarms” incited by one or several virgin

queens. Colonies issuing too many afterswarms can be left

queenless and all but empty. Meanwhile, other colonies are

embattled as virgin queens stand their ground and fight to

the death, rather than fly and flee. Whichever adventure is

chosen dictates how soon a colony will begin its enchanting

story of the season (or come to a dwindling end).

When a virgin queen emerges victorious, she next faces

a perilous journey and must brave the outside world

alone in pursuit of her maturity. If one thing can stand

between her and her crown, it’s the weather. Not long after

emerging, the virgin queen takes an orientation flight. On

the afternoon of the sixth day, if the weather is warm and

calm, she is ready for takeoff. She will take an average of

three mating flights and a deadly courtship with a dozen to

sixteen drones. This new queen will start laying eggs a few

days after her last date.

We’re also exploring whether treatment and requeening can

eventually work together. One idea I have is the large-scale use

of protected queen cells across entire loads in conjunction with

our spring and/or late summer formic programs. Exactly how the

“mechanics” of this might work isn’t clear to me yet. Does the

protected cell go in before treatment, during it or afterwards? Will

the virgin replace the old queen, fail, or potentially coexist for a

period?

I don’t know yet. But if a commercial operation can efficiently

produce thousands of cells, providing every colony with an oppor-

tunity to introduce young, selected genetics is an exciting IPM tool

to explore. There are a lot of unknowns, particularly with formic.

I remember telling some fellow commercial beekeepers late last

summer that I “trialled” formic on 16 loads, they were all laughing

because they thought I nuked all my queens. I just laughed with

them and said if the queen was too old to survive, that’s good, as I

don’t want to waste my time with her through winter only for her

to fail when I need her most, going into almonds.

Autumn: preparing for the next lap

Eventually the circle comes back around.

Honey comes off. Brood begins contracting. We are finish-

ing building the bees that must carry colonies through winter and

propel us into pollination and beyond.

Our vertical barrier system becomes particularly valuable here.

After a trip to New Zealand in 2017, we deliberately developed

a system that clearly separates our honeycomb from our brood

equipment. Different combs and frames allow us to remove the

honey crop and get colonies back to a defined brood nest.

That gives us a genuine honey-free treatment window.

Apiguard is one treatment we’re interested in using during that

post-honey autumn period where appropriate. In late autumn, as

brood approaches its seasonal low, oxalic dribble becomes another

tool of particular interest to us.

Then we’re approaching the shortest day again. And the circle

starts another lap.

Where I want the circle to take us

Longer term, success for me isn’t adding more treatments to

the circle. It’s hopefully removing as many as possible. I’d love to

reach a position where mite numbers are low enough going into

winter that we’re not opening hives unnecessarily and putting

treatments in during the lead-up to almonds.

Likewise, I want bees entering summer with low mite levels,

young queens and good nutrition so that we can leave them alone

as much as possible while they’re on peak honey flows.

Treat hard when we need to. Monitor relentlessly. But create

periods where the bees can simply be bees.

Resistance will take Bayvarol and possibly Apivar completely

out of our program. Other products will move around the circle

as we learn more. Genetics should become increasingly important

in jumping off the treatment treadmill. Pollination will continue

changing our calendar. And that’s all okay.

The circle isn’t the treatment program. The circle is the

framework that allows the treatment program to change.

Draw your own circle. Perhaps the most useful thing about

all those messy whiteboards is that my circle definitely shouldn’t

be your circle.

A beekeeper in Queensland will draw a very different one to

a New South Wales beekeeper. Another Victorian commercial

beekeeper may have completely different honey flows, pollination

contracts, queen-rearing windows, nutrition, brood cycles and phi-

losophy towards treatments.

So rather than publishing “my circle” as a recipe, I’d like to offer

it as a signpost.

Start with a blank circle. Mark your longest and shortest days

and your equinoxes. Add your pollination jobs. Add your honey

flows. Add your queen-rearing window. Think about brood and

nutrition. Identify the periods when you want to leave your bees

alone.

Then overlay your monitoring.

Only then start writing treatments onto it.

Treatments will change. Resistance will change them for us.

Seasons will change. Our queen breeding will change. Pollination

will change. Unmanaged honey bees will be decimated and prob-

ably leave us scars to remember them by. Things I’ve written on

this year’s whiteboards will undoubtedly be rubbed off next year.

But the circle will remain. Shortest day. Almonds. Spring.

Other pollination jobs. Honey. Queens. Summer. Nutrition.

Winter bees. Autumn. Brood contraction. Endless monitoring.

There isn’t really a varroa season anymore.

There is just the next “continuous” lap.

KEY MESSAGES

• Varroa management is 365 days a year. Think in cycles,

rather than individual treatments.

• Monitoring leads the program. Never assume a treatment

has worked.

• Put the bee before the treatment. Brood, nutrition, queen

age and colony strength belong on the circle.

• Plan around the business. Honey flows, pollination and

operational capacity determine what is actually possible.

• Resistance changes everything. Preserve treatment options

and expect the program to evolve.

• Young queens and genetics are part of IPM.

• Draw your own circle. The framework is transferable; the

treatment recipe isn’t.

Code of Practice updated

By Rod Bourke

The Australian Honey Bee Industry Biosecurity Code of

Practice (the Code) was developed and nationally endorsed by all

Australian beekeeping member bodies of the Australian Honey

Bee Industry Council (AHBIC), which includes the ABA, around 10

years ago. Since then, it has been widely adopted by many states

including NSW as a condition of registration. This means, that as

a beekeeper (someone that has even just one bee colony) you are

legally required to be registered and to follow the management

guidelines as set out in the Code.

The Code provides a clear management framework for

Australian beekeepers to apply best practice biosecurity measures

on every hive. A broad range of people with varying personali-

ties and skill levels like to keep bees, so the Code is a guideline to

ensure that everyone manages their bees to a certain standard.

This aims to reduce the biosecurity threat to the beekeepers own

bee colonies as well as to all others around them. Biosecurity is

a shared responsibility, so every beekeeper has an obligation to

do the right thing and reduce the opportunity for risks to emerge

from within their own operation.

Many beekeepers tend to get swept up in the charm of bee-

keeping (and selling a bit of honey) and may over time get a bit

carried away with the number of hives they actually have buzzing

around the place. When this is tied in with life’s many other com-

mitments including family, work and recreation the bees often end

up getting far less attention then they require or deserve. This can

also mean that the care provided is less than that outlined in the

Code as the bare minimum they should be receiving.

If you are not following the Code’s level of hive management

then it is likely that you will be getting far more swarming, failing

queens, non-productive, diseased, slimed or wax mothed hives

than you should be, resulting in a lot less honey per hive. You

Honey Trace was recently tested end-to-end under simulated

conditions by an Australian beekeeper and a large commercial

Australian honey packer. A simulated sales order was shipped

and tracked to a Southeast Asian distributor and retailer where it

was followed to a consumer in Singapore. The project has been

demonstrated and presented at state apiary association confer-

ences and field days in NSW, Victoria and the Northern Territory.

Geelong Beekeepers Club Inc.:

Where Knowledge Gets Shared

By Nathan Whitford, President

A club that nearly wasn’t

Our story starts at the turn of the millennium. Club records differ

on whether the first meeting was held in 1999 or 2002, but the

reason for it is not in doubt. At the time, the only beekeeping

meetings were held in Melbourne by the VAA’s Melbourne Section,

which meant a trip to the city for anyone in Geelong who wanted

to learn from other beekeepers. Founding member John Edmonds

set out to form a club closer to home. Not everyone was convinced

the region could support one. Early meetings drew just 12 to 15

beekeepers, and John paid the first year’s hall rent of $200 himself,

hoping the club would take off. That small group of new beekeep-

ers and seasoned professionals, meeting monthly to learn and net-

work, is where today’s club began.

Like many community groups, we have had our wobbles. In

2014 a run of changes almost caused the club to fold. Gary

Montgomery stepped in as President and formed a new commit-

tee. He shifted the club’s focus to be more

inclusive of recreational beekeepers, who

remain the heart of our membership.

That shift shaped the club we are

today. Gary was instrumental in starting

three things we still rely on: our meeting

format; the Ask a Beekeeper sessions,

with experienced beekeepers on hand to help newcomers; and the

Beekeeping Essentials Course. He also helped start our association

with The Heights, a National Trust Victoria homestead in Newtown

dating back to 1854, where the club still keeps its own teaching

hives.

Founding members John Edmonds and Stan Filippis were

made Life Members in 2022, followed by Gary in 2024 and Helmut

Woerner in 2025 for his years of mentoring, technical help and out-

reach. Our Life Members say a lot about the club: people who give

their time and knowledge to others.

Education is what we do best

Each meeting opens with an Ask a Beekeeper session, where mem-

bers can put any question to a panel of experienced beekeepers.

This is so popular we run a beginner and experienced beekeep-

ers session simultaneously in two different rooms for 45 minutes

before the general meeting. Participants always have to be told

when it’s time to cut off the discussion and convene. The general

meeting begins with a monthly biosecurity segment – a member

survey helped us settle the running order and start times.

The Beekeeping Essentials Course is the front door for anyone

thinking about taking up beekeeping. One doesn’t need to be a

member to enrol, but the course includes a year’s club member-

ship, so new beekeepers have the club’s support through their

first year. In 2025/26 we ran four, in August and October 2025

and February and March 2026. All were fully booked. We encour-

age new members who want handson practice to come along to

our hive openings at The Heights. These smallgroup sessions are

designed to help build confidence in a supportive, relaxed setting.

It’s a great chance to see how a hive works, ask questions and

get comfortable with the basics, and a good “try before you buy”

opportunity if you’re still deciding whether beekeeping is for you.

Our club maintains a strong library of resources to support your

beekeeping journey: recorded guest speaker presentations, prac-

tical guides, biosecurity updates and a full archive of past news-

letters. Financial members are welcome to borrow from the club

library at no cost.

Every two years we host a book sale featuring some of the best

beekeeping books at up to 40% off the recommended retail price

(cheaper than QBD and Amazon). Plus, our website offers a fuel

discount program that anyone can use, even nonmembers.

Punching above our weight

For a volunteer-run club, we are lucky to attract some of the best

speakers in the business. In the past few years we have hosted

dozens of presentations, from Agriculture Victoria (AgVic) biosecu-

rity staff and Varroa Development Officers to university research-

ers, commercial beekeepers and international guests.

Recent highlights include Dr Jody Gerdts, a Benalla-based

queen breeder and researcher who has studied Varroa-resistance

breeding programs in the United States; Cormac Farrell, head bee-

keeper at Australian Parliament House, on

urban and rooftop beekeeping; Dr Kenya

Fernandes, a University of Sydney microbi-

ologist who explores how forest health and

bee health shape the antimicrobial prop-

erties of honey; and Dr Scarlett Howard of

Monash University, who studies bee intel-

ligence and cognition.

We have also made a point of showcasing women in beekeep-

ing and bee science, and of bringing a diverse range of voices into

the room. Alongside Drs Gerdts, Fernandes and Howard, we have

heard from Dr Nadine Chapman on Plan Bee, Australia’s honeybee

breeding program; Dr Shona Blair on the medicinal properties of

honey; Tina Sebestyen on integrated pest management; and Ally

Driessen on biosecurity.

Our biosecurity line-up has been just as strong. AgVic’s Adam

Maxwell has outlined the National Bee Pest Surveillance Program;

former Varroa Development Officers Scott Denno and Kris Fricke

have briefed us on monitoring, cultural control and organic acids;

and VAA Vice President Dr Andrew Wootton has spoken to us on

hive splitting and, recently, the Bawden Assay as an alternative to

the alcohol wash.

Our speakers have also taken us well beyond Geelong, with

talks on beekeeping in Washington State, Uganda and elsewhere

in Africa, the black beech honeydew of New Zealand, and the

Apimondia congress. Some of our best sessions still come from

our own members, including John Edmonds on honey judging and

the season ahead, Stan Filippis on queen rearing and winter pack-

down, and Stephen Fridey on summer hive maintenance and pre-

paring for Varroa. They stand up and share what has worked, what

hasn’t, and what they’d do differently.

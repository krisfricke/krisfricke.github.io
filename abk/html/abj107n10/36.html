John Edmonds’ Monthly

Diary, September 2026

T

he beekeeping landscape here in southern Victoria has

changed a lot in the last fifty years due to canola. In the

1970s some farmers at Balliang were growing rapeseed and

my father would shift his hives to the flowering crops. They flow-

ered in October and the bees bred rapidly and made 20 kg of water-

white honey that tended to candy quickly. A super of foundation

combs would be drawn and filled. We would use Demaree boards

to make a split to reduce swarming and raise a new queen on top

of the hive. The bees would become nasty and every hive that was

opened would sting you. In those days protection was scant: no

gloves, just black netting veils and your work clothes, or we started

wearing white boiler suits. A new beekeeper accompanied us to

learn about beekeeping; he kept his hands in his pockets as he

was frightened of the bee stings. The first job was to go along the

fence posts and catch the swarms, as every hive would swarm. The

change occurred when the Canadians bred a better plant for the oil,

with low acid content, and called it canola. Nowadays it flowers in

southern Victoria as early as late July or August, and by the end of

September it will be finishing, except for the odd late crop. So, our

season starts much earlier. Prior to canola, capeweed [Arctotheca

calendula] flowered in mid-September and October and was the

first plant to build up the hives to a swarming fever. It made an

unpleasant-flavoured honey which spoilt the nice yellow gum

[Eucalyptus leucoxylon] honey.

My observation is that canola needs to flower on warmer days,

greater than 18 degrees, and the ground needs to be dry, to yield

nectar that increases the hive weights. Even so, excellent breeding

will occur as copious amounts of pollen are harvested. The prob-

lem locally is that the cold south winds off the sea hamper the bees’

foraging.

Large areas of roadsides and unused land have been covered in

the yellow flowers of soursob [Oxalis pes-caprae]. This plant seems

to be expanding and the bees collect pollen and a thin nectar

from it.

September 11th - Despite a nice warm day and no wind, the

hive scales have only reported an increase at one crop. All the

other four canola crops are still only slightly up or down each day,

yet the hives are breeding well and eating into the honey stores to

make more brood and many drones.

I have just finished putting out the first queen cells for the

season. I have waited for the weather to warm up. The overwin-

tered nucleus hives have been split, and with the canola midway

through flowering I expect to see some nice laying queens at the

end of September. The last two springs were frustrating as cold

windy days interfered with the mating flights.

September 13th - A nice warm 24 degrees but a strong after-

noon wind. The hive scales again showed topsy-turvy results: gen-

erally the hives increased in weight, but the two crops that had

been consistently the best recorded losses. Both crops are at peak

flowering. At one site the canola may be finishing, but faba bean

crops further away are yielding, as the pollen is in the frames.

Swarming has started, especially in hives that have not been

attended to. Extra supers and splitting away a couple of frames of

brood and adding foundation will prevent swarming.

It is a good idea to split your hives during the spring build-up to

prevent swarming, but most importantly to have replacement nucs.

New South Wales beekeepers have found they need a third of their

hive numbers in reserve to make up for varroa-generated losses.

Plantain has its first flowers and an almost white pollen will be

gathered.

Capeweed is prolific in some paddocks and the first flowers

have started but need to develop the pollen stamens before the

bees will work it.

Pelargonium, known as cranesbill, has the small mauve flowers

opening.

Paterson’s curse [Echium plantagineum] is flowering better this

year due to the good rains. My friend’s scale hive in the Riverina

reports increases of 1 to 2.5 kilograms per day.

Sweet pittosporum [Pittosporum undulatum] is flowering and

the bees love the flowers. This tree tends to be a weed in many

places.

Wattles of various species are flowering throughout the bush-

lands. It appears the flowering time is delayed for sallow [Acacia

longifolia] and prickly acacia.

Coastal teatree [Gaudium laevigatum, formerly Leptospermum

laevigatum] has the first flowers and is often found with bearded

heath [Leucopogon parviflorus], which breeds the bees very

quickly. Beekeepers report their hives at Point Lonsdale have filled

with honey, most likely teatree.

Yellow gum and red box [Eucalyptus polyanthemos] are bud-

ding and flowering through central Victoria. Nectar is being gath-

ered and the flowers are full of nectar.

Grey box [Eucalyptus microcarpa] is making growth that looks

promising for next autumn.

Ironbark [Eucalyptus tricarpa] at Bendigo is growing new tips;

could it flower in successive years?

Sugar gum [Eucalyptus cladocalyx] is growing rampantly, but

once again it is hard to tell whether it will flower next autumn or

the following year. Various stands of trees appear to have different

stages of bud development.

The almond farms are pleased with the pollination this spring,

with a good set of nuts.

September 21st: As the country starts to become dry, the dry-

land canola crops are finishing flowering. The colour of the flowers

turns from bright yellow to a golden hue. Although the bees still

work the flowers, the yield drops off quickly, especially after the

strong hot north winds experienced over the weekend.

Beekeepers are reporting that the canola has not made honey

like previous years. The bees breed well with little weight gain. Is

it the varieties of canola? Or the cooler weather during flowering?

Or lack of ground moisture? The hives are very strong and will now

need to be shifted to another flowering source.

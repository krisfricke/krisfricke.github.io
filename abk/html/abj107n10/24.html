There is a trade-off.

We still want heavy hives and plenty of stores going into winter.

A short period on those flows can be commercially valuable and

Our first lap around the circle

While we detected mites in September 2025 and monitored to

what I would call a “fairly good” standard, January 2026 was really

the beginning of our first full lap. The “denial” period had to end

and we had to start with the reality of putting in treatments.

We started with Bayvarol, but were met by what I can only

describe as a great big wall of honey.

Our bees had done exceptionally well on my late mum’s favou-

rite honey, the mighty river red gum Eucalyptus camaldulensis.

That’s normally a pretty good problem to

have, but suddenly we had thousands of

hives carrying a huge amount of honey and

we needed access to the brood nest, fast!

We were a little late getting Bayvarol

into some loads. Not only were we faced

with the awkward dance of getting past

the most welcome wall of honey, we had

the added logistical effort to move the hives off the rivers and onto

summer-flowering ironbark, brood populations were contracting

and in places our mite washes moved into double figures!

In hindsight, it was almost a textbook demonstration of why

the circle matters. Everything was happening at once: honey pro-

duction, nutrition, brood contraction, operational workload and

increasing varroa pressure.

Alongside Bayvarol we trialled a single

pad of Formic Pro treatment across 16

loads as a temporary measure while honey

was being removed and the Bayvarol team

caught up.

That was my introduction to using

formic. We observed beautiful brood

rearing following treatment, particularly,

and unusually, as the brood expanded on ironbark bees. I think

the summer rains helped the Chinese scrub Cassinia arcuata yield

timely pollen and helped the bees expand, a happy coincidence.

My observation was that the formic put the bees into a

hygienic frenzy and subsequently the bees opened the brood nest,

unpacking all the “bee bread” stored from spring flows and the

river red gum. We also observed a reduction in field bee numbers

after formic treatment. But those same loads have subsequently

developed into strong, healthy colonies through winter and into

spring 2026.

It wasn’t without a field force loss: adult bees with varroa feed-

ing holes couldn’t bear the brunt of the formic and perished. But it

gave us another tool and, importantly, more treatment experience.

Bayvarol ultimately worked extremely well for us, and while it

was a marathon to get all the treatments in and the treatments

out, it ran parallel with the winter bee production period. After

the treatment period we were seeing zero mite counts across all

loads, mites became undetectable.

That was then. The situation has changed remarkably quickly.

Resistance changes the circle

In 2026, resistance to both major synthetic chemical groups

was confirmed in Australia. Agriculture Victoria has now con-

firmed some Victorian varroa mites resistant to the pyrethroids

Bayvarol and Apistan, as well as the formamidine products Apivar

and Apitraz.

That doesn’t mean every mite in Victoria is resistant. Our own

experience demonstrates why monitoring matters. Both Bayvarol

(summer-autumn) and Apivar (winter) produced very low mite

counts, largely undetectable. But neither product can simply be

assumed to work anymore. Bayvarol may work one more time for

us, Apivar may be something we can return to semi-regularly into

the foreseeable future. The experts say that with a good organic

centric program, formamidine products can still be “reasonably”

effective. With Bayvarol it’s a switch that is permanently turned

off.

And that is probably the biggest change resistance makes to

my circle: monitoring leads the treatment program, which is now

largely organic based treatments. Organic treatments have no

known mite resistance; however, they bring their own set of

challenges.

The national guidance similarly recommends monitoring

before and after treatment and rotating control methods rather

than relying repeatedly on the same

chemistry.

In my apiaries, our tolerance for

allowing increasing mite numbers is going

to be very low through this acute transi-

tion period of the next two seasons. We

have treatment stocks on hand, we’re

monitoring extensively, and we want to

know whether something has worked, not simply assume it has

because we put it in.

Winter, almonds and lessons from year one

After the 2026 shortest day we began our pre-almond pro-

gram.

Most of our loads that were honey super free received Apivar

while simultaneously being assessed for

hive strength. The plan was to have that

treatment period dovetail with almonds

and remove the strips as the bees came

off almonds.

We also trialled Aluen Cap on loads

that still had honey supers on. The mite

results from those Aluen Cap loads were

excellent in early spring, predominantly zeros with the occasional

“The best honey flow for the

beekeeper isn’t necessarily the

best pollen flow for our bees.”

How the VAA has been advised

From the VAA Chemical Working Group’s September 2026 update,

following its meetings with Agriculture Victoria’s chemical regulation team:

“Victorian beekeepers are now managing varroa as an ongoing

part of their business. Miticide resistance has been detected

in Australia, including Victoria. This means access to effective,

practical, and legally compliant treatment options is increasingly

important.”

“The Working Group has confirmed with Agriculture Victoria that

Victorian beekeepers may use single pad applications of Formic Pro

as part of a complete treatment.”

“The Working Group has also confirmed with Agriculture Victoria

that Victorian beekeepers may use a half-dose Apiguard treatment

option.”

“As varroa management evolves in Australia, maintaining access

to a range of effective treatment options, while supporting

responsible chemical use, good record keeping and integrated pest

management, will remain critical.”

– Australian Bee Journal, September 2026, p. 10

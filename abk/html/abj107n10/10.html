Breeding the Honeybee

By Gill Greenstein

T

hanks to Bruce Diggles, VAA Melbourne Section Librarian,

for choosing this book. Before you read the review, here is a

potted history of Brother Adam.

Brother Adam was born in 1898 and died in 1996, and in between

he spent over 70 years breeding and evaluating Buckfast bees.

He was sent to the Benedictine monks at Buckfast Abbey in

England at the age of 11 owing to poor health. In 1915 he began

assisting the abbey beekeeper and started his beekeeping appren-

ticeship. One year later in 1916, 30 of the 46 black bee colonies the

abbey had were devastated by Isle of Wight disease.

During those formative years Brother Adam’s experiences influ-

enced his thinking and gave birth to the idea: “What if we could

breed a honey bee that combined the best characteristics of dif-

ferent races?”

He travelled extensively, visiting more than 70 countries and

observing different bee traits, from bees in the oases of the Sahara

Desert, such as A.m. sahariensis, across Europe, Middle East, North

Africa, to the slopes of Kilimanjaro, collecting bees, noting traits like

wing power, spring development, tongue reach, resistance to dis-

ease and pests and so on.

As part of Brother Adam’s research, he installed an isolated

breeding station on Dartmoor, to obtain selected crossings. If you

get the opportunity to visit, don’t forget your umbrella. The rainfall

is twice that of the rest of the country.

Brother Adam sadly passed away at in 1996 at the age of 97

years, at the time the oldest English Benedictine monk. During

his lifetime he received two honorary doctorates, the Order of

the British Empire (OBE), and the Order of Merit of the Federal

Republic of Germany (the Bundesverdienstkreuz) for his scientific

work with bees.

Brother Adam wrote three books:

• In Search of the Best Strains of Bees

• Beekeeping at Buckfast Abbey

• Breeding the Honeybee

And “Breeding the Honeybee,” is definitely a book to have on

your shelf. I found it fascinating from the perspective of a colony

owner, rather than just a queen breeder. It looks at the colony as

a whole, considering the qualities and traits that are influenced by

both the environment and genetics, and how these can ultimately

benefit the beekeeper. For me, this is what makes the book so

interesting. It encourages you to look beyond the queen and con-

sider the colony as a whole.

“The whole is greater than the sum of its parts.” Thanks,

Aristotle!

Brother Adam’s book is divided into three parts:

• Theory of Breeding

• Practical Possibilities in Breeding

• An Evaluation of the Breeding Possibilities of the Different Races

of Honey Bees

Brother Adam takes you through the genetics. This was a chal-

lenge! I haven’t done much with genetics since I was studying my

O-levels, and I don’t think that covered haploid, diploid and par-

thenogenesis.

Then there’s that great little quote:

“A drone has no father and no sons, only grandfathers and

grandsons.”

After a late-night session brushing up on chromosomes, genes

and alleles, and lots of conversation with my study buddy, “Chat,” I

ploughed on through Brother Adam’s book.

Brother Adam’s book also prompts you to look at hive health,

good management practices and what you actually want from

your bees. Granted, Brother Adam had 70 years –and access to a

car! – To explore thousands of miles through Europe, the Middle

East and North Africa, studying and collecting different bees with

the qualities he was looking for: temperament, disease resistance,

foraging ability and so on.

He also had the time to explore different types of genetic

breeding, whether it was nature’s way, pure breeding, line breed-

ing, cross breeding or combination breeding. He spent years evalu-

ating, documenting and scoring the results.

And I think he probably bred a lot of bad-tempered bees along

the way, which he then had to breed out! For many of us, having

the right bees for production is an economic goal. For others, it’s

simply having bees that don’t sting the hell out of your dog, your

neighbours and you!

Brother Adam really does provoke you to think about the pri-

mary and secondary qualities you want in your bees, and the quali-

ties that influence management.

There’s a great example of this in a Dutch research team Dr Judy

Gerdts mentioned during a conference talk, about their bee-breed-

ing work. They bred what they called “get-down bees,” bees that

would get down the frame when you lifted the lid. I thought that

was brilliant!

So, I recommend this book. It’s definitely one to have on your

shelf. It gives you a great insight into genetics and bee breeding

and, more importantly, it gives you plenty of food for thought.

I would probably read it alongside the NSW Department of

Primary Industries’ Queen Bee Breeding material.

So, for me:

Five honey pots – one for your bookshelf.

And I think it’s a book you’ll come back to again and again!

Honey Pot Rating:

Outstanding – Every beekeeper should read it.

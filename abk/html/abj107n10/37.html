A farmer told me that they will spray the canola with a desiccant

and harvest it while standing. The reason is that cockatoos and

parrots decimate the windrowed crop.

My friend is pollinating two new varieties of canola for seed

production at Hay. These varieties have never been grown here

before. One variety yields huge amounts of pollen, with bees

coated in pollen as if they had been on pumpkins.

September 27th - The cold southern wind stopped the hives’

weight gain, and many of the canola crops appear to be almost fin-

ished flowering and yielding nectar.

Bogong moths [Agrotis infusa] have started to migrate, and if

they come south, they will affect the flowering of the eucalyptus

trees. The moths walk over the flowers at night, leaving a smell

that repels the foraging honey bees. Many good honey flows of

yellow gum, red box and yellow box [Eucalyptus melliodora] have

been stopped in the past.

September 28th – Today I was pleased to inspect two hives

at Bellbrae. It has become a hotspot for varroa after beekeepers

shifted bees with varroa into the area in March. In June the hives

averaged 50 mites. I added oxalic acid strips. After winter, in August,

I found the mites had dropped to 20. Now, in late September,

one of the hives had swarmed and made a new queen. During

the brood break the varroa numbers had dropped to 8 mites. The

second hive had filled with more honey and needed more room.

The count was 9 mites in the wash, and uncapping drone brood

found no mites. I am happy the oxalic acid strips are containing the

varroa. The strips have been chewed, but enough remains to be

effective. Replacement strips will be installed next month.

I noted an absence of New Holland honeyeaters [Phylidonyris

novaehollandiae] around my property. This week many have

moved in and are feeding on the bottlebrushes [Callistemon spp.]

and Melaleuca viridiflora. They must migrate in from unknown

areas. Shortly, hundreds of them will be here nesting and chasing

each other in flocks.

Varroa

I found two five-frame nucleus hives to have one and two varroa

mites respectively. Oxalic acid strips were placed in them. The

one with two mites had strips placed as staples. The one with one

mite had one strip laid on top of the frames. An inspection three

weeks later found the nucleus with the staple strips had only one

mite after uncapping all the drone brood. The nucleus with the

strip lying on top of the frames had 10 varroa mites, so clearly that

didn’t work; an extra oxalic acid strip was added and both strips

were placed as staples.

While placing oxalic acid strips into a hive and uncapping drone

brood, I was wearing nitrile gloves. I found an unmarked queen. I

held her with the gloved fingers and marked her. When I released

her, I was surprised to see a bee immediately grab and attempt

to sting her; I had to intervene and hopefully prevented her from

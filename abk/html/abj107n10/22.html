Varroa:

Going Full Circle

One full year living with varroa

By Lindsay Callaway,

President, Victorian Apiarists’ Association

Proudly mounted on my office wall is our first varroa

“captured” by my diligent lead apiarists Greg and Lewis,

2nd September 2025. Yes, likely to have been “delivered”

on almonds and yes, varroa is a fact of beekeeping life we

endure now.

Since varroa arrived, I’ve spent a lot of time standing in front

of a whiteboard drawing circles. Not out of madness, but out of

curiosity and a desire to understand how this little beast fits into

the circle of my beekeeping life.

There must be twenty versions by now. Some neat. Most

definitely not. Treatments get written in, crossed out and moved.

Honey flows appear. Almond pollination window is a staple fea-

ture. Queen rearing gets squeezed in. Brood levels rise and fall.

Mite numbers change. Then more information arrives, normally

by collaborating with fellow commercial beekeepers and then I

start drawing it all over again. Like a sniper adjusting his scope, it

gets sharper and sharper in its focus.

But the basic idea has remained the same.

Varroa is now with us all year, every year. There is no longer

a start date and no finish date.

You could represent that with a straight line stretching forever.

An infinity symbol would probably be even more accurate. But for

me, a circle works better because it gives

the year context, rhythm and structure.

It allows me to see the seasons. It lets

me overlay honey flows, pollination ser-

vices, brood cycles, nutrition, queen rear-

ing and the times of year when we want to

leave the bees alone.

And then, importantly, I can overlay

where the varroa treatments fit best, work best for my operation

and my bees. The result isn’t a perfect treatment plan. It is a

planning tool and one I keep refining as we learn the dark art of

managing varroa.

Start with four points

My circle starts empty. I first mark the shortest day, longest

day and the two equinoxes. Those four points divide the year

into quarters and immediately give the circle some context. Then

I start adding our beekeeping year.

August gets blocked out for almond pollination. In our oper-

ation, pollination increasingly extends beyond almonds into

September and October. This year, around three-fifths of our bees

have continued from almonds into other pollination work. I see

this window growing both in terms of volume of hives and length

of pollination time as the unmanaged “naturalised” Apis mellifera

gets wiped out by our “foe,” varroa.

Then comes honey production. For us in Victoria, the broad

honey window is from spring through to late autumn.

Then add queen rearing. For us, that window runs roughly

September through to late February or early March. After that,

cooler weather and falling drone populations start closing the

queen-rearing door, as does the time needed to get the new hives

established pre-winter, strong enough to “show up” to almond pol-

lination on August 1st.

Already the circle is becoming a useful visual tool and we hav-

en’t even put a varroa treatment on it.

Now put the bee on the circle

The next layer is what is happening biologically inside the hive.

When is brood expanding? When is it contracting? When are

we building our long-lived winter bees? When is nutrition critical?

When are we likely to have our lowest brood levels? Where are

the varroa hiding or in some cases not hiding?

Varroa development follows the seasonal development of its

host. As brood and adult bee populations decline, the proportion

of mites on adult bees can rise sharply. The national Australian

IPM guidance identifies late summer and autumn as the hive pop-

ulation-decrease phase, the period when

colonies are producing overwintering

bees while varroa is completing its final

population growth phase – yes, it wants

to survive too.

That makes late summer and early

autumn a critical part of my circle. This

is where nutrition becomes every bit as

important as the treatment program.

Australia has some enormous advantages here over, for exam-

ple, our friends in the US and Canada. We have extraordinary

nectar and pollen resources and, in many areas, opportunities to

position bees onto good nutrition when they need it most, while

they are producing their “winter” bees. We don’t need horrible

tankers of sugar and half-baked patties of nutrition when we have

great access to resources.

But there is also a lure for commercial beekeepers. A big

honey crop isn’t necessarily the same thing as good bee nutrition.

Flows such as ironbark Eucalyptus sideroxylon (Red Ironbark) and

Eucalyptus tricarpa (Ironbark), grey box Eucalyptus microcarpa

and blue mallee Eucalyptus polybractea can produce plenty of

honey, but they aren’t necessarily where I want bees sitting for

on the circle first. Put the

treatments on last.”

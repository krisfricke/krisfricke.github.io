A friend is confused. He split his apiary and shifted half to

yellow gum and red box. He was surprised to discover a canola crop

within flying distance. He has been “vaping” the hives each week.

Inspection found low levels of varroa in the shifted hives, but the

hives remaining at the faba beans, which have received the same

vaping treatment, have high numbers of varroa. Must be reinva-

sion, but from where? No other known apiaries are nearby.

Another friend has taken their hives back home to northern

NSW from the almond pollination and found they have large num-

bers (70 per wash) of varroa in the hives. She applied a formic

flash treatment but the varroa numbers were still higher after

the treatment.

When I uncap drone brood, I used to return the dead drones

to the hive, as the bees will eat them as protein. Then, when I

started to find the odd varroa, I decided to discard the dead larvae

on the ground rather than risk possibly recycling the mites. I won-

dered what would eat the pupae. At a bee site with meat ants

[Iridomyrmex purpureus] the larvae disappear as fast as they hit the

ground. At another bee site I discovered a crow had been watch-

ing, and he would follow me around eating the pupae and dead

bees he could find.

I have found bee hives with no varroa amongst infected hives.

Kris suggests they may just be lucky not to have been infested yet

as it unevenly spreads through the apiary.

The Geelong Beekeepers Club has been very active, making

a set of presentations covering the various topics to educate its

members. The next project is getting members and other beekeep-

ers to record the varroa levels and discover hives that have reduced

or no varroa. Then these strains of bees will be bred from, and

the queen cells distributed to club members. The hope is to breed

bees that adapt to the local environment and are varroa resistant. I

expect this project will take time.

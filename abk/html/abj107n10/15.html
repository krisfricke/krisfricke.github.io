BEE BITS

Varroa mites escape the evolutionary trap of haplodiploidy

It was previously believed that male varroa mites, like honey

bees, only received one set of genes (were “haploid”) which

exacerbated the already extreme inbreeding caused by their

usual mating with their sisters. Eliash et al followed 30 mite

families over three generations and sequenced the whole ge-

nome of 223 individual mites. What they discovered is that the

male mites retained both sets of their mother’s genes (diploid

arrhenotoky). While the females have one set of genes from

their father and one from their mother, the males have two sets

from their mother) The male passes a random half of his genes

to his daughters.

This slows down the loss of genetic variation and increases

the “effective population size,” which in a species that has gone

through evolutionary bottlenecks both in host jumping and

geographic spread is very relevant.

Haploidity had been referred to as an evolutionary trap be-

cause once male-haploidity develops there’s no evolutionary

pressure for them to become diploid again. Varroa somehow

developed from having fully haploid males (as most of its sister

species do) to this “functional diplodiploidy.”

As you will know in bees any unfertilized egg becomes male

and any fertilized egg becomes female. It is unknown what de-

termines whether a varroa egg develops into a male or female,

though unmated females can still lay male eggs.

This finding is particularly relevant to the development of

resistance in varroa mites. Under pure haplodiploidy the genes

are bottlenecked through males every generation, limiting the

preservation of recessive genes. This newly discovered mecha-

nism changes that dynamic. As to specifically how, good or bad,

that is beyond the scope of this short summary.

Read the original paper:

Eliash et al. (2026) “Varroa Mites Escape the Evolutionary Trap of

Haplodiploidy” Nature Communications 17: 10392.

Hive Density

Dear Editor,

How many beehives can I have on a lot

of land? I can’t seem to find an answer to

this simple question.

*James

Dear James,

Ah this is one of the great unanswerable

questions of the universe, like how long is

a piece of string? And what’s the sound of

one hand clapping?

But let’s try to answer this question

by looking at what the natural carrying

capacity of the environment is, that

is, how many “wildly occurring hives”

(“feral colonies”) occur per square

kilometre. Before we even begin looking

into that it will be obvious that different

environments will have different answers,

but nevertheless we must do our best so

we carry on. Conveniently a recent paper

Visick 2023 did a lot of the legwork for us

by compiling data from a whole bunch of

earlier surveys. In their survey of surveys

they found feral colony density to vary from

0.1 to 24.2 hives per km2, and specifically

among the 16 studies pertaining to

Australia the average was 4.4. The areas

surveyed in Australia were all in the south-

east, so we’re not talking about the vast

outback deserts of the interior here.

Now we run into a second indeterminable

variable, bees don’t generally confine

themselves to one square kilometre,

so we must account for their foraging

range, which can vary widely. They

prefer the closest food they can get, but if

particularly desperate they might fly as far

as 7 kilometres, but to not completely lose

our minds let’s take the very commonly

referenced honey bee range of five

kilometres. From our 100 metre garden

plot these bees flying five kilometres have

a territory of 31.4 km2 to forage across,

which is to say we can imagine we are

concentrating all the hives-per-kilometre of

that entire range in one spot and it should

work. So 31.4 x the Australian average of

4.4 should give us 138.

When doing any kind of extrapolative

maths the next step is to stop and think

does that look realistic or have we gone off

the rails here, and I would say that does

make sense, when you keep in mind these

naturally occurring colonies may not be thriving but just getting by, and remember your

hives are competing with any other hives anyone else has AND the naturally occurring

feral colonies already in the area, so if we scale back to a fraction of that, I think most

places with a temperate climate can sustain 10 to 20 hives at a single location.

One other concern immediately occurred to me though: the density of survey numbers

could be limited not by available forage but available nest sites. An individual survey of

occupied artificial nest boxes in near Wagga Wagga NSW (Cunningham 2022) performed

between 2010 and 2014 found that where artificial nest boxes had been provided in

small remnant yellow box (Eucalyptus melliodora) woodlands, there were 69 colonies per

km2 and that’s not even counting any that weren’t in the artificial boxes – far in excess of

the earlier 4.4, though that was only 15.1% of the provided boxes so perhaps they were

maxing out the local carrying capacity at this higher rate.

But now consider the range of answers we’ve already obtained: 0.1/km2 would

give us 3 hives in our central location, 24.2 would give 760; the numbers obtained by

Cunningham would give 2167, and that wasn’t even full yellow box forest but small

sections amid mostly cultivated land. In the almonds they put at least 2 hives per acre,

which works out to 500 per km2, x the 31.4 km2 foraging range that would be 15,512

if one were to concentrate them all in one place (which would be a terrible idea). So

where does this leave us? With the rather exasperating result that we can put somewhere

between 3 and 15,512 hives on our 100 metre garden plot? If we’ve proved anything it’s

just how unanswerable this question is.

But I think there’s still an insight there, which is that (A) on the low end there’s a

realistically limiting factor. If you live in some desolate location that looks to be on the low

end start out with two hives and slowly build up, you’d be surprised the places that can

support ten hives.

And (B) on the higher end it’s usually more hives than you can manage as a beginner.

In lush rural and dense hobby farm areas if you can find a place to put 20 hives go for

it (but work your way up to that, first and second year beekeepers often have a horrific

loss rate).

The suburbs can support a surprising number of hives, often more than rural areas,

due to every quarter acre house lot having a flower garden, but here you’re seriously

constrained by space and it would usually be pushing the bounds of propriety to have

more than two in a suburban house block.

Where there’s a big flow on and commercial beekeepers are swarming to the location

you may find a 150 hive apiary every few kilometres down the road.

The bottom line is that this question truly has no easy answer other than learning

from experience, but if you’re just beginning you can always start with two and go from

there.

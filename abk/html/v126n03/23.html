THE CHEMISTRY AND PHYSICS OF FORMIC ACID

Understanding its unique properties has helped scientists

and beekeepers determine how to best use formic acid as a

miticide.

One of the biggest advantages of formic acid is that it is

not lipophilic18. Formic acid does not stick to fat molecules—

which means that it does not stick to beeswax, helping

beekeepers avoid unwanted residues in their hives. Because

it is produced by plants, formic acid is also naturally present

in honey19. Consequently, Formic Pro is one of only two mite

treatments legal to use in Australia during nectar flows, with

honey supers in place.

This is key, considering formic acid is used as a fumigant

treatment. Almost all available miticides are contact

chemicals, meaning they depend on individual bees to

physically interact with them to work, which can take time.

By contrast, formic acid evaporates readily inside of honey

bee hives, permeating the entire cavity and beginning to act

immediately and without requiring direct contact with each

bee/mite. Relative to other treatments, Formic Pro treatments

begin and end over a much shorter time period, with

treatment being complete after one week instead of several.

MOVING FORMIC ACID VAPOURS

THROUGHOUT THE HIVE

In a process called diffusion, formic acid molecules

evaporate away from a Formic Pro strip out into the hive

cavity. The rate of diffusion is determined by temperature,

how tightly molecules are packed together (density), and

the size of the molecules20. As one of the smallest organic

molecules, formic acid diffuses very efficiently, even making

it through the microscopic pores of the sealed brood cells21

in quantities sufficient to kill varroa mites22 — a clear

advantage over contact treatments, given that this is where

much of the varroa population is found23.

The behaviour of the bees collectively, because they flush

out excess vapours via fanning, plays a crucial role in the

dose of formic acid delivered to the colony. Consequently,

applying formic acid in colonies that are small or weak can

be risky. For Formic Pro, the minimum colony size is 10,000

bees – around 5-6 fully covered Langstroth frames. NOD

does not recommend using Formic Pro in nucleus colonies.

The size and shape of the hive cavity are also important

considerations when using formic acid. If the hive cavity is

too small, the risk of the bees being overcome by excess

vapours increases. NOD does not recommend Formic Pro

treatments for hive cavities smaller than full-sized Langstroth

(or similar) brood chambers. In the case of a single brood

chamber colony only, NOD recommends adding a honey

super with frames during the treatment to allow the colony

to expand away from the vapours more easily.

Its gaseous nature means that formic acid treatments are

temperature-sensitive — both to lows (below clustering

temperature) and highs (above brood-rearing temperatures).

Partly, this is because formic acid evaporates at different

rates as the temperature changes, and honey bees only

tightly thermoregulate the brood-rearing zone. However,

the behaviour of the bees also changes below clustering and

above brood rearing temperatures24. These two factors can

interact in complex ways, leading to unpredictable results.

Thus, to ensure the treatment performs as intended, Formic

Pro should be applied when the daytime temperature highs

range between 10 °C and 29.5 °C.

Formic acid vapours are heavier than air, so for maximum

efficacy, Formic Pro strips should be placed in hives above

frames with sealed brood. See the Formic Pro application

overview for details17. And because bees within the hive fan

out excess vapours, it is important that hives have sufficient

ventilation that is lower than where Formic Pro strips are

placed (e.g., keep lower entrance fully open).

On the other hand, beekeepers also need to be mindful of

providing too much ventilation, as is the case in hives with

screened bottom boards with the inspection tray removed.

In such instances, the formic acid vapours dissipate before

they reach the necessary level25 of at least 20 ppm inside the

hive to begin to kill varroa26. NOD’s internal studies show

that levels of formic acid around 40 – 80 ppm over several

days are ideal to achieve effective varroa control.

OTHER NOTABLE FACTS

Once the Formic Pro treatment period has ended, the strips

become inert. Unlike other varroacides, Formic Pro strips do

not need to be removed from the hive, and can be left in

place until the next inspection. Strips are fully compostable

upon their removal at the beekeeper’s convenience.

Queen issues after formic acid use are often reported

by beekeepers. However, across the 13 studies conducted

on Formic Pro to date that recorded queen data, ranging

from internal NOD studies to independent, peer reviewed

academic papers, there was no statistically significant

difference in queen issues between Formic Pro treated

colonies and colonies that had not received any mite

treatment (data available upon request).

Finally, it is worth mentioning that formic acid can be

corrosive to metal. Corrosion is most evident when formic

acid comes in direct contact with the galvanized metal used

in some types of equipment, such as when strips of Formic

Pro are placed under galvanized queen excluders. However,

in most instances, the degree of corrosion is low, and can be

easily removed from affected equipment with minimal effort.

MODE OF ACTION OF FORMIC ACID

But what does formic acid actually do to kill varroa mites?

Although still a matter of some speculation, decades of research

in other organisms and some new studies on gene expression

in varroa mites and honey bees provide multiple streams of

evidence that formic acid disrupts arguably life’s most basic

processes: the production of a molecule called ATP27.

First, recall that formate (formic acid dissolved in water) is

a key component of biochemistry, but that organisms tightly

regulate formate levels. This is almost certainly because

too much formate interferes with cellular respiration28 — a

series of chemical reactions carried out by all oxygen-using

organisms that generate the energy, in the form of ATP,

required to power the processes of life.

While the specific details of cellular respiration vary

depending on the organism, all versions rely on the

movement of electrons by a protein called cytochrome c

oxidase29. Excess formate blocks cytochrome c oxidase30,

shutting down cellular respiration31. Without the ability to

produce the energy it needs to run, the organism dies.

However, if this were the whole story, then formic acid

would be as toxic to bees as it is to mites.

It is often said that the dose makes the poison32. That is,

for every substance, there exists a dose small enough to not

be harmful, and a dose large enough to cause damage. Even

essential substances, such as water33 and oxygen34, are deadly

at high enough doses. Determining a toxic dose depends

on the specifics of how your body deals with the substance

in question. The differences in physiology between species

means that different organisms have different tolerances for

different substances. This simple biological truth underpins

how we deal with all infectious disease: we use a treatment

(e.g., an antibiotic like penicillin) that is worse for the

disease-causing organism (e.g., a bacteria) than it is for the

host (e.g., us). But make no mistake: If you ingest enough

penicillin, and you too will experience penicillin toxicity35.

Recent evidence suggests that the difference in tolerance

to formic acid may lie in the details of how varroa mites and

honey bees regulate formate. Both varroa and honey bees

use a family of proteins called Cytochrome P450s36 to convert

formate to less harmful substances. Exposure to formic

acid affects the production of these proteins by varroa and

honey bees differently37. The reason for the difference is not

known. It may have to do with the fact that honey bees are

likely to encounter higher levels of formic acid in the foods

they eat (honey19, nectar, and pollen); whereas the levels

encountered by varroa mites feeding on honey bee fat bodies

and hemolymph are likely to be lower. Or it could be for

some other reason entirely. At this point, there is not enough

evidence to definitively support any specific explanation.

FORMIC ACID: A SUCCESSFUL BEEKEEPING

SOLUTION FROM NATURE

Varroa mites pose a significant challenge to beekeepers

around the globe. Treating for varroa while maximizing

honey production and minimizing impact to the colony is a

delicate balance. The unique properties of formic acid offer

beekeepers a natural way to target varroa mites while leaving

no harmful residues in honey, wax, or hive components.

Beekeepers and scientists have determined the optimal

dose of formic acid that effectively controls varroa while

leaving the colony unharmed. When properly applied,

formic acid evaporates easily, allowing vapours to penetrate

all areas inside of the hive, including under brood cappings.

Bees play an active role in formic acid applications, flushing

out excess vapours.

With the help of products such as Formic Pro, beekeepers

can now more easily harness the power of formic acid for

use in their own hives.

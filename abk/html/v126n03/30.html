There is an increasing interest in the use of such smart

hives in Australia. The first companies from New Zealand and

Australia are starting to roll out their offers to beekeepers

nation-wide. We have so far identified 16 companies offering

smart hive systems, seven of them are based in Australia

(HiveIQ, FatBee, Apis Prime, BeeStar, ApisAus Pty Ltd,

BeeMate, Apis Solutions) and one more has a representative

in Australia (BeeHero, USA/Israel). These either already

provide hive sensors or have advanced prototypes in hand.

Another international company, BroodMinder (USA), is

directly sold in Australia by The Urban BeeHive.

We interviewed and surveyed beekeepers across Australia

to determine their needs for hive sensors. Beekeepers were

quite positive toward the use of sensor technology; however,

all beekeepers mentioned that the high costs of sensors are

a main issue, as well as connectivity problems for those

already using sensors. Despite the initial positive response,

our interviews and online survey also revealed that, at this

stage, sensor technology is not widely used in Australia. We

asked beekeepers what aspects of beekeeping they believed

sensors should help manage. Unsurprisingly, beekeepers

indicated that the prevention of colony death was the

parameter they expected the most from equipping hives

with sensors. Mirroring the responses from the interviews,

detecting diseases was very important to beekeepers,

followed by swarming and robbing detection.

We also reviewed the scientific literature to determine

what metrics of interest to beekeepers could be derived

from the vast dataset harvested. We found that while some

companies already provide estimates of the number of

frames of bees or of bee health or bee activity, sometimes

using Artificial Intelligence, science is still in the process of

validating the interpretation of hive sensor data. Here is

what the scientific literature has to say about the different

sensors, in short:

Temperature: bees regulate temperature to around 34.5°C

to optimise brood development from spring to autumn, and

around 10°C in regions with cold winters. Thermometers

can measure how well they control the temperature, which

can provide an estimate of the numbers of frames of bees in

the hive, of the presence of brood (workers don’t heat the

hive in the absence of brood), and of general hive health.

A wide range of sensors can be placed within beehives to turn them into “smart hives”

and monitor them remotely. These sensors usually measure the weight, temperature,

humidity, sounds or vibrations, and bee activity. Sometimes, they come with GPS units

to locate hives and prevent theft. This data is then sent to the internet via wifi, cellular

or satellite connections, allowing beekeepers to access these metrics in real time.

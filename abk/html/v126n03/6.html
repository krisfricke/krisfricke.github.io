Varroa! What About

Unregistered Treatments?

An Opinion by Mike Allerton

As varroa spreads throughout NSW,

now into VIC and soon into QLD,

beekeepers are turning to unregistered

treatments to save their bees and their

businesses.

In my role as a contract trainer for

the Varroa Response in NSW, I was not

permitted to talk about unregistered

treatment products. It was not in the

curriculum and we could only talk

about legal options. At every Varroa

Workshop, the elephant in the room is

Oxalic Acid!

Now that the workshops in NSW

have come to a close, I am free to share

my observations and ideas.

I’ve been told by some industry

insiders that there have been well over

one million oxalic acid (OA) strips sold

into NSW, either as kits or ready-to-use.

There have also been various models

of sublimation/fogging devices sold.

Clearly the demand for OA is high and

people are prepared to break the law to

use this effective, inexpensive option.

As biosecurity officer of Amateur

Beekeepers Australia (ABA), I am

frustrated by our legal inability to offer

training in the safe effective use of OA

and other non-synthetic treatments

that are available in other countries. My

fear is that without training, someone

may injure themselves which may lead

to OA never gaining registration.

I look to New Zealand for inspiration,

our nearest varroa infested neighbour

and what do I find? Over 12 years

ago, a group of passionate beekeepers

successfully lobbied their government

to include an “Own Use Exemption”

clause in the legislation.

What is an “Own Use Exemption”

clause? First let’s understand the

Australian legislation. I’ll make it simple

because I’m not a lawyer. Any chemical

substance used to treat livestock or

crops against pests and diseases is

regulated by the Australian Pesticide

and Veterinary Medicines Authority

(APVMA). The APVMA is empowered

by the Agricultural and Veterinary

Chemicals Act – the law. This ensures

that our food (honey) is safe and that livestock (bees) and crops are healthy.

Chemical treatments are submitted to the APMVA for registration where the

product is subjected to rigorous testing to ensure it is effective and safe. This is

an expensive and time-consuming process. Some suppliers choose not to register

their product because they don’t see they will recoup their costs in the small

Australian market.

The “Own Use Exemption” clause would allow beekeepers to use non-synthetic

treatment options that have been proved elsewhere for efficacy and safety.

They’re not permitted to produce and sell or market any product to others. It

is strictly for use on their own bees. Product that falls within the scope of the

proposed clause include OA, formic acid, hops and essential oils. There may be a

naturally occurring product yet to be discovered.

In New Zealand it has led to innovative delivery methods and more efficacious

dosing that would never make it through the registration process. For instance, it

has been proved by the University of Florida and others that 2g, 3g and 4g doses

of OA are far more efficacious than the registered dose of 1g which is largely

ineffective.1

I proposed to the ABA that we lobby government to amend our legislation in

a similar way to New Zealand. The proposal was endorsed and we took it to

the AHBIC AGM this year to gain the support of our fellow member bodies. It

was accepted that AHBIC will support our growing movement to change the

legislation.

The Crop Pollination Association of Australia (CPAA) had already taken steps to

promote “Own Use Exemption” by conducting a survey of its members to gauge

support. The CPAA supported our proposal at the AHBIC AGM and offered to

extend their survey to ABA members and share the collected data.

The survey recently closed with a healthy response rate. The results show

overwhelming support for the clause.

I’ve received comments suggesting that if the clause is implemented, suppliers

won’t bother registering their products, restricting the range of available

treatments. I spoke with several suppliers who already have skin in the game.

The response was consistent. With New Zealand as a good model, they see that

most beekeepers will stick with off-the-shelf products, so there will always be a

market. The beekeepers that the clause covers are already operating outside of

the registered product range. The clause will simply allow them to continue to

protect their bees legally.

Where to now? Research scientist from Southern Cross University, Dr. Cooper

Schouten, has offered his support in providing scientific data supporting the

use of various non-synthetic treatments. Randy Oliver has offered to write a

supportive document based on his ongoing varroa treatment research. The ABA

will be distributing letters for beekeepers to send to their local federal member. I

will meet with politicians to explain the benefits of the clause and hopefully gain

their support. Keep an eye out for a petition to parliament. We now have a

Facebook Group, Own Use Exemption Clause for Aussie Beekeepers, where we

will provide information and progress updates.

With enough noise, the squeaky wheel is oiled. The “Own Use Exemption”

clause is that oil.

Mike Allerton

Biosecurity Officer

Amateur Beekeepers Australia

Western Australia. First flowers are appearing on various wat-

tles: hedge [Acacia paradoxa], sallow [Acacia longifolia], golden

[Acacia pycnantha], and Cootamundra [Acacia baileyana]. My

grandmother was born mid-August and her father would cut

branches of flowering golden wattle from the You Yangs, where

they lived at Station Peak, and bring them into the house to

14/8/2025 As I came up the Geelong Ring Road I noticed

the hills of soursob [Oxalis pes-caprae] in flower with the but-

ter-yellow flowers. Every year we see more of this weed grow-

ing around Geelong. It provides the bees with a thin amber

nectar and tan-coloured pollen. The bees breed well on it,

but sometimes they can get nosema from the thin nectar. If

the farmer decides to plough the paddock, the bulb has many

small bulblets attached and it will spread and multiply.

21/8/2025 I added an ideal box to the hives working canola.

A good shake of nectar and plenty of pollen. Lots of brood and

pleasing to see drone brood nearly ready to emerge. Adjoining

the crop is a massive paddock of small canola which should

be a month away from flowering. We desperately need more

rain. In mid-September the bees will need nucs taken off them

or else they will swarm.

Onion grass [Romulea rosea] pink flowers are just commenc-

ing flowering. The bees gather tan-coloured pollen and a light

brown nectar and breed profusely. Often the cockatoos can be

seen eating the bulbs from under the ground.

Along the surf coast I noticed the sallow wattle flowering,

coastal bearded heath [Leucopogon parviflorus] just starting to

flower. This plant makes horrible-flavoured honey but makes

the bees breed in profusion. Golden wattle through the bush

north of Lorne is in full flower. Blue gums [Eucalyptus globulus]

around Lorne and surrounding bush are flowering, and the

cockatoos are having a great time nipping off the flowers and

the tips of the foliage.

1/9/2025 I have been told that the almond trees near Mildura

had only a light flowering this year and the strong winds have

blown many petals off the flowers. It is expected that by the

middle of the week the message to start shifting the bees out

will begin.

It is interesting to see the Facebook clips of beekeepers

bringing large loads of bees from Queensland and New South

Wales to the almonds. They certainly must have big areas for

their trucks to manoeuvre and load the hives. Many of the big

trucks and trailers would not fit through my farmers’ gates or

bush sites.

Around Ballarat the canola crops are still not flowering and

have healthy large cabbages, which will result in more flowers.

Good rains will ensure good crops.

The late canola crops that have been sown are just plantlets

(2 to 4 leaves). It will be interesting to monitor when they start

flowering, hopefully after the main crop has finished. When

having successive canola flowerings, you have to be careful as

the bees will swarm. Best to take away a nucleus of bees off the

strong hives and give them frames of foundation. Locally it has

been too dry and crops are not as good as normal. Hopefully

some heavy rainfall will come soon.

It is amazing to see how quickly the canola crops catch up

and start flowering. The best-yielding crops are those that

grow a large “cabbage” before flowering. I added boxes and

foundation frames to some hives today, and despite the cool

showery weather the bees had fresh nectar in the combs. My

Iraqi refugee worker commented, “the bees are fat and will fly,”

in other words the boxes are full of bees and will swarm.

The CVAA conference at Maryborough celebrated the 60th

year of the association. It was a very good conference and

well attended. A novel approach of being free to members

was very good. The catering was exceptional. Frank Lindsay

from New Zealand gave a presentation of his experiences with

varroa in New Zealand and made recommendations that the

Victorian government should allow the use of alternative varroa

treatments for the beekeeping industry to remain viable. The

first three years will require constant vigilance monitoring the

mite levels in the bee hives. After the feral and unmanaged

bee colonies have died then you should be able to control

the mites with two treatments per year. Overseas has seen

approximately half of the beekeepers and bee hives drop out

after varroa invasion. It appears the effect on the bee hives is

different in each area, as the many variables have to be consid-

ered. Small hive beetles are proving to be a nuisance in north-

ern NSW but as a result the feral hives are dying out quickly.

An interesting comment from Frank Lindsay’s speech was

there will be many swarms when varroa comes, as the bees

abscond, but they are full of bees carrying varroa. Best to kill

them. After the feral hives die out, the swarms generally do not

hang in the trees and shrubs but fly straight to new homes in

buildings and hollow trees. Eventually they die out from the

varroa.

Many beekeepers have been making their own treatments,

but this is not advised. It is easy to make a mistake and possibly

kill or weaken your hives. I know a beekeeper who imported an

oxalic acid vaporizer and, despite wearing a full-face mask with

the correct respirator, he was alarmed to find he could smell

the vapours. It will be easy to make a mistake and damage

yourselves. Initially it is best to use the approved treatments,

although they are expensive. They are safe and reliable.

The only honey flow for the season that looks certain is red

gum [Eucalyptus camaldulensis]. Hopefully other trees will

make budding.

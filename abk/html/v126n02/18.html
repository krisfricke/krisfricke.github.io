This year’s QBA conference and 120th AGM it was

hosted at the Rydges Southbank Conference Centre in

Townsville, the perfect winter escape with warm sunny

days. The theme was “Sustainable Beekeeping Beyond

Varroa”. Attendance was down compared to last year,

but the mood was positive. Jo Martin, QBA secretary said

it’s been a challenging year for QLD honey producers and

crop pollinators who depend on access to NSW to sustain

their business. The NSW Varroa incursion is affecting

QLD beekeepers even before mites cross the border. A

number of hobby beekeepers from the south-east said

they couldn’t justify the travel expense this year, but plan

to attend next year.

The AHBIC AGM was also hosted at Rydges the day

before the conference. It was a full day with many updates

and topics discussed. Stephen Fewster retired from the

executive board after 20 years of dedicated AHBIC service.

The QBA conference had updates on the Varroa

response and where QLD fits into the program. Rob

Stephens, Biosecurity Officer from the QLD Department

of Agriculture & Fisheries (DAF) spoke of the Asian honey

bee incursion in Brisbane. He said there was no sign of

Varroa on the euthanised bees and is confident that the

incursion will be contained. Rob said DAF in conjunction

with recreational and commercial beekeepers, has set

up 18 sentinel hives along the QLD-NSW border from

Coolangatta to Stanthorpe. Each hive is strategically

located in high-risk areas near the NSW border and

integrated into existing apiary sites where beekeepers

volunteer a single hive for sentinel purposes. These

hives are checked by DAF personnel every six weeks

using sticky mats placed in the brood box with miticide

strips for 48 hours. The mats are then submitted to the

Biosecurity Sciences Laboratory (BSL) for analysis to

detect any mites. The sentinel hives offer the advantage

of early mite detection in specific geographical areas.

This initiative exemplifies the successful collaboration

between recreational and commercial beekeepers and

DAF, united in the mutual aim of early Varroa detection,

ultimately benefiting the industry as a whole.

Professor Roberto Garcia shared the Argentinian

50 years of Varroa experience. His main message for

Australian beekeepers is to breed for Varroa resistant

bees from our own stock rather than importing genetics,

either semen or queens from overseas. He also promotes

the use of organic treatments and warns of the risk of

over-use of synthetic treatments. Mites quickly became

resistant to flumethrin (Bayvarol) in Argentina, and it

soon became ineffective. Prof. Garcia emphasised the

importance of integrated pest management.

Dr. Diana Leeman spoke of the potential of

entomopathogenic (insect harming) fungi use against

Varroa. There’s a long research path ahead, but the

potential for chemical-free Varroa controls is exciting.

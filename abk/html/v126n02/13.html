Make sure she has space! Every hive should have 2-3

super on top, but space for new brood.

While I try really hard not to give beekeeping advice, the

one pro tip I harp on is making sure your workers have

space to do the jobs we want of them. It is the one common

problem we have all the time, and the number one issue

most of us ignore.

The American Honey Producers Association (AHPA)

is working hard on the HIVE Act (Honey Identification,

Verification and Enforcement), an anti-dumping lawsuit,

and prevention of tallow eradication — just to name a

few items. Progress on ELAP (Emergency Assistance for

Livestock, Honey Bees and Fish) and funding for USDA are

also on their list of successes. For a small group they have

a large footprint, and some good progress for the industry.

That said, we agree we are still way behind where we

should be. The AHPA has one full-time lobbyist and the

American Beekeeping Federation a partial one. By dollar

value comparison, an industry our size should have at least

five working on our issues.

So let me be clear: Our industry, despite the great efforts

of a few, is way behind the ball in my personal opinion,

and that is not in any way in opposition to our current

organizations. These groups, both or all of them if you like,

are a lot of fantastic people over the last decades, trying to

move the industry forward. I have supported them more

than most over the last years, and think we all should

continue to do so.

I could name some of the fantastic people who have

been and are working on industry projects, but in doing

so it would leave out so many more behind the scenes and

over the years. This is a team effort over time, it will have

wins and losses, but the goal is to always have a winning

season. If we work together, and admit our failures, we can

succeed.

My April article was directed at benchmarking other

industries. Unfortunately, maybe I didn’t explain that

process clearly. The goal is to look at the other industries

and compare ourselves to them: What are they doing that

is helping them win? What are the indications of winning?

As mentioned, similar-sized organizations have dedicated

people and money in the bank, things we do not have as

an industry. These are metrics I picked; others may have

different ideas. If we are unable to look at the leaders such

as the Almond Board, and emulate their success, we are

doomed. Take that as negative if you must, but to do better

we have to discuss the problem first. That’s not negativity

to me, it’s honest evaluation. I also sincerely believe it, and

am not afraid to say it. This is not grade school, there are

no participation trophies awarded.

Unfortunately, there are still several large voids to be filled

despite the best efforts. For example, take Tropi mites. Is

there a central plan? T-mites have been on the radar for

over 10 years. Were it not for Sam Ramsey studying them,

and then sharing his knowledge, we would have not have

known of their rapid spread. What is the containment plan?

Within the states at the moment, we have no real plan

in place. APHIS tells us they have a plan, but they cannot

share it with beekeepers, and I am quite sure it is woefully

inadequate. I know of several individuals who are working

on things, and sounding the alarm, but as a task for one of

the groups it’s barely mentioned.

This is just one example. There are many more things

that should be led and organized by our industry, but are

simply not front and center. We as an industry, with small

membership and a large footprint, simply do not have the

bandwidth at the moment. Take practical research into

things like bee food. In any agricultural industry animal

nutrition is well known and understood, and we are still

way behind the curve. For the most part our knowledge is

still based on work done in 1953. Is our industry actively

working on better research? The answer is primarily no.

For an industry to move forward we need dedicated

leadership. We simply cannot keep running on the backs of

beekeepers who volunteer for a couple of years. There are

two reasons for this; the first is that those of us who step

up also have to do our day jobs. For example, last year I

spent a lot of time on behalf of one of the organizations to

work on a couple of issues. I have to ask, is that part of my

losses? Did I take my eye off the ball? Had I not spent those

hours working on FDA and border issues, would I have

decided to do more mite checks? I can tell you that most

past presidents of the organizations will tell you that your

day job suffers a lot. A good portion of that is based on the

organization of those groups, and reliance on members to

do those tasks as well as their day job.

The other big problem is turnover; every couple of years

the leadership changes. This last change was huge, 100%

turnover in tasks and who was/ is working on them. Several

of us who were working on things have been pushed aside,

and new people are trying to get up to speed. Just as that

happens, there will be another change. No other successful

agricultural group that I am aware of is run this way.

We at some point soon need to address these problems

if we intend to grow or even remain stable as an industry.

If we are to remain viable, we need groups that spend

more time on issues than on planning the next convention.

This is the nature of our industry — something we

desperately need to get a handle on. We absolutely need

to figure out how to convert all the fundraising and

research dollars to actual valuable tasks and political wins.

As we as beekeepers are driving around this summer

pulling honey and checking on things, we really need

to be thinking about these issues if we want to see this

industry grow and thrive. Figure out how you can support

the organizations, and jump in!

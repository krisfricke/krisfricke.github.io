At a time when honey adulteration devalues our

premium product, Georgia Moore from the University of

Sunshine Coast gave us a figurative taste of her work

to authenticate Australian honey varieties. Georgia

is building a honey “fingerprint” database using

phenolic profiles that offer reliable, repeatable honey

authentication and identification.

I asked local recreational beekeeper Lynton Wright about

his thoughts on Varroa. As a retired science teacher, he

was looking for the connection between beekeepers and

scientists. He’s happy to have found it at the conference.

Lynton is confident that beekeepers armed with quality

science and good beekeeping practices can successfully

manage Varroa.

Chris Anderson from NSW DPI gave an update on the

NSW Varroa experience. He said we can expect mites to

spread like a tidal wave, with massive reinfestation rates.

Good beekeeping practices will allow us to surf the wave

until Varroa stabilises. Poorly managed hives will perish

due to Parasitic Mite Syndrome and increased small hive

beetle loads.

The available organic treatments alone have proved

inadequate to cope with the high reinfestation rates

during the initial unstable period. Chris said that until

Varroa stabilises it will be necessary to use synthetic

treatments in rotation to knock the heavy mite loads

down. He estimates from the data that it could take three

years to stabilise.

On a brighter note, Leisa Sams from the Stanley River

Branch hosted a guided honey tasting evening featuring

some of QLD’s finest varieties. Leisa is a graduate of the

Advanced Course with the Italian National Register of

Experts in the Sensory Analysis of Honey. The tasting was

magnificently paired with creations from celebrity chef,

Matt Golinski.

The gala dinner was also prepared by Matt Golinski

carefully crafted with a range of QLD honey varieties. It

was a taste sensation.

Seasonal Update

John Edmonds · Pages 8–9 · October 2025

SEPTEMBER 2025

What happens to our food supply if we don’t have honey bees?

Many years ago, this fellow Cliff Winkler lived in Torquay. He was a prominent local and would write many letters to the editor of the local paper, generally stirring up the “greenies.” I had 50 hives of bees in his “depot,” a piece of land on the edge of Torquay where he would dump clean fill.

One day he approached me saying he needed bees in his large backyard as he was not getting his pumpkins pollinated. I declined his offer as I said the 50 hives were about 1 kilometer from his house and I thought they would do the job. He came to my place and counted the pumpkins set on my father’s vines and then went next door and counted my neighbor Kevin’s pumpkin vines. He concluded he needed bees. So, I took 15 strong hives and placed them along his back fence. Behind the property was the Surfcoast Shires work center and beside was the Ambulance building so I expected complaints. The bees flourished and became triple deckers full of honey. Every time I attended to the bees Cliff insisted on being tough and watched me inspecting the bees from close quarters refusing to wear a veil until eventually the bees forced him to retreat. As the pumpkin vines died off, I could not believe my eyes as the backyard garden was covered in pumpkins. They certainly benefited from the bees nearby. No complains arose and the bees were kept there for many years until Cliff passed away and the property was sold. The depot and house were demolished and replaced by a new housing development shopping complex.

My neighbor Kevin would keep the seeds from his butternut pumpkins. My father who resided next door decided to grow some Jap pumpkins. The resulting pumpkins the following year were a mix of the two varieties. Kevin was not impressed with having green stripes on his butternut pumpkins.

August 12th 2025 While shifting bees to flowering canola, I observed an excellent germination of clover plants. The farmer does not like clover as it can cause bloat in the cattle. During the dry autumn she fed her herd with purchased hay, as a result the Balansa clover seeds were ingested and passed through the cows and spread nicely throughout the paddock. “Corkscrew,” also known as Pelargonium [Pelargonium spp.], has started to flower with its pink flowers. Plantain [Plantago lanceolata] is in profusion and when it starts to flower it provides a lot of pollen for the bees.

The season appears to be two to three weeks later than usual. Almond trees at Mildura are barely flowering. The ones locally are in full bloom and they are usually three weeks before the almond orchards of the Riverland. It is expected the almonds will flower into the first week of September this year. Bee hives have been brought in from many places—New South Wales, Queensland, Northern Territory—and a South Australian orchard purchased two B-doubles of bees from Western Australia. First flowers are appearing on various wattles: hedge [Acacia paradoxa], sallow [Acacia longifolia], golden [Acacia pycnantha], and Cootamundra [Acacia baileyana]. My grandmother was born mid-August and her father would cut branches of flowering golden wattle from the You Yangs, where they lived at Station Peak, and bring them into the house to celebrate her birthday.

14/8/2025 As I came up the Geelong Ring Road I noticed the hills of soursob [Oxalis pes-caprae] in flower with the butter-yellow flowers. Every year we see more of this weed growing around Geelong. It provides the bees with a thin amber nectar and tan-coloured pollen. The bees breed well on it, but sometimes they can get nosema from the thin nectar. If the farmer decides to plough the paddock, the bulb has many small bulblets attached and it will spread and multiply.

21/8/2025 I added an ideal box to the hives working canola. A good shake of nectar and plenty of pollen. Lots of brood and pleasing to see drone brood nearly ready to emerge. Adjoining the crop is a massive paddock of small canola which should be a month away from flowering. We desperately need more rain. In mid-September the bees will need nucs taken off them or else they will swarm.

Onion grass [Romulea rosea] pink flowers are just commencing flowering. The bees gather tan-coloured pollen and a light brown nectar and breed profusely. Often the cockatoos can be seen eating the bulbs from under the ground.

Along the surf coast I noticed the sallow wattle flowering, coastal bearded heath [Leucopogon parviflorus] just starting to flower. This plant makes horrible-flavoured honey but makes the bees breed in profusion. Golden wattle through the bush north of Lorne is in full flower. Blue gums [Eucalyptus globulus] around Lorne and surrounding bush are flowering, and the cockatoos are having a great time nipping off the flowers and the tips of the foliage.

1/9/2025 I have been told that the almond trees near Mildura had only a light flowering this year and the strong winds have blown many petals off the flowers. It is expected that by the middle of the week the message to start shifting the bees out will begin.

It is interesting to see the Facebook clips of beekeepers bringing large loads of bees from Queensland and New South Wales to the almonds. They certainly must have big areas for their trucks to manoeuvre and load the hives. Many of the big trucks and trailers would not fit through my farmers’ gates or bush sites.

Around Ballarat the canola crops are still not flowering and have healthy large cabbages, which will result in more flowers. Good rains will ensure good crops.

The late canola crops that have been sown are just plantlets (2 to 4 leaves). It will be interesting to monitor when they start flowering, hopefully after the main crop has finished. When having successive canola flowerings, you have to be careful as the bees will swarm. Best to take away a nucleus of bees off the strong hives and give them frames of foundation. Locally it has been too dry and crops are not as good as normal. Hopefully some heavy rainfall will come soon.

It is amazing to see how quickly the canola crops catch up and start flowering. The best-yielding crops are those that grow a large “cabbage” before flowering. I added boxes and foundation frames to some hives today, and despite the cool showery weather the bees had fresh nectar in the combs. My Iraqi refugee worker commented, “the bees are fat and will fly,” in other words the boxes are full of bees and will swarm.

In the August 2025 American Bee Journal, Christine McDonald has an interesting article about juvenile hormone. [see “Juvenile Hormone” by Christine McDonald in this issue]

The CVAA conference at Maryborough celebrated the 60th year of the association. It was a very good conference and well attended. A novel approach of being free to members was very good. The catering was exceptional. Frank Lindsay from New Zealand gave a presentation of his experiences with varroa in New Zealand and made recommendations that the Victorian government should allow the use of alternative varroa treatments for the beekeeping industry to remain viable. The first three years will require constant vigilance monitoring the mite levels in the bee hives. After the feral and unmanaged bee colonies have died then you should be able to control the mites with two treatments per year. Overseas has seen approximately half of the beekeepers and bee hives drop out after varroa invasion. It appears the effect on the bee hives is different in each area, as the many variables have to be considered. Small hive beetles are proving to be a nuisance in northern NSW but as a result the feral hives are dying out quickly.

An interesting comment from Frank Lindsay’s speech was there will be many swarms when varroa comes, as the bees abscond, but they are full of bees carrying varroa. Best to kill them. After the feral hives die out, the swarms generally do not hang in the trees and shrubs but fly straight to new homes in buildings and hollow trees. Eventually they die out from the varroa.

Many beekeepers have been making their own treatments, but this is not advised. It is easy to make a mistake and possibly kill or weaken your hives. I know a beekeeper who imported an oxalic acid vaporizer and, despite wearing a full-face mask with the correct respirator, he was alarmed to find he could smell the vapours. It will be easy to make a mistake and damage yourselves. Initially it is best to use the approved treatments, although they are expensive. They are safe and reliable.

The only honey flow for the season that looks certain is red gum [Eucalyptus camaldulensis]. Hopefully other trees will make budding.

John Edmonds is a beekeeper of many decades, having started in beekeeping by helping his Dad, Murray, who kept hives in the Gippsland region of Eastern Victoria. John’s beekeeping expanded in 1982 when he formed a partnership with Murray. Nowadays, John manages approximately 500 hives across Western Victoria, producing different varieties of honey, rearing queen bees, and selling a range of beekeeping equipment.

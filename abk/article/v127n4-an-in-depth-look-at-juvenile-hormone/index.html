An In-Depth Look at Juvenile Hormone

Christine McDonald · Pages 10–12 · October 2025

This one is going to get nerdy! This was the research project that I completed to present as part of my Master Beekeeper examination series at Cornell. I will do my best to make this very academic topic engaging and applicable so please read on. I promise you’ll learn a fact or two that will impress even the most experienced beekeeper at your next club meeting.

Juvenile hormone (JH) was first and best understood for its role in metamorphosis. Honey bees do undergo metamorphosis and, unsurprisingly, the role of JH in this process closely mirrors what is seen in butterflies, beetles and ants.

But what is unique and interesting about JH in honey bees is that it revisits adult honey bees and acts in ways completely unrelated to metamorphosis. In adult bees, we see JH levels influencing the transition from nurse to forager, the productivity of the hypopharyngeal glands, queen differentiation, and drone transitions leading up to flight.

I will go into more detail and relate this to some common behaviours we see in bees, but first it’s interesting to see where this hormone is produced and how researchers were able to study its impact by controlling levels of JH in living adult bees.

Juvenile hormone is produced in the corpora allata, located in the head of the bee. It can be removed from live bees to test the impact on bee behaviour in the absence of JH, and applying measured concentrations of a JH analog (just like the real thing!) topically to live bees. JH Acts on Queen Differentiation

In the larval phase, levels of JH are key in the differentiation of queen and worker larvae. Since either a worker or a queen could result from any single fertilized (female) egg, It appears that JH has an important role in making a queen a queen, and stopping a worker from developing into a queen. Higher levels of JH in queen larvae are associated with more ovary filaments, while a low level of JH in worker bee larvae leads to programmed cell death in the reproductive organs (De Souza, Hartfelder, and Tarpy, 2019).

JH Influences the Division of Labour

Juvenile hormone levels are lowest in nurse bees, and highest in foragers. Sullivan et al. (2000) noted that this holds true even for early foragers and reverted nurse bees, so the relationship is not simply a factor of age but related to the act of foraging (or some part of that act - spoiler alert!). When bees had their corpora allata removed completely, they would still eventually transition to foraging, so JH levels are not the only factor, but they are a strong one. Foraging can be induced in bees of different ages through topical application of juvenile hormone.

Higher levels of juvenile hormone also make bees better guards. Bees treated with a topical application of a JH analog were more reactive to alarm pheromone than bees with lower levels of JH (Schulz, Sullivan, and Robinson, 2002).

JH Induces Physiological Changes

In addition to behavioural changes, a higher level of Juvenile Hormone in adult bees also prompts some physiological changes. When honey bees are young nurse bees whose primary role is to feed brood, their hypopharyngeal glands are food conversion machines, turning bee bread into brood jelly. As they age and transition away from nursing, the function of their hypopharyngeal glands changes. Instead of producing jelly, they begin to produce the enzymes necessary to process nectar. This is fascinating enough on its own, but even more fascinating is that this process can be accelerated or reversed by manipulating the levels of juvenile hormone in the individual bee (Schulz, Sullivan, and Robinson, 2002).

JH and the Act of Flight

In adult workers, levels of juvenile hormone are lowest in early adulthood and highest in late adulthood, as they transition to out-of-hive jobs like guarding and foraging. The opposite is seen in adult queen bees, where the highest levels of JH are observed in early adulthood (when queens take their mating flights) and are lowest in late adulthood. This finding would suggest that juvenile hormone might somehow be related to the act of flight itself.

The experience of drones supports this idea. JH does not show any correlation with the rate of sexual development or maturation of drones, but it does set the pace for behavioural development. It is lowest in young drones, and rises as they move to the periphery of the hive and as their diet switches from brood food (also fed to young adult drones) to honey. Juvenile hormone levels in drones peak just as they start to take their first mating flights (Harano, 2013).

JH Impacts Behaviour, but What Impacts JH?

We’ve seen how levels of juvenile hormone can impact honey bee behaviour and physiology, but that was all based on human manipulation of the hormone level in adult bees. So the more interesting question is what can impact JH levels (and thus influence these changes) in the real world?

A number of studies have sought to answer this question by looking at single factors independently. It’s interesting to note that some of these results seem to be in contradiction with each other and there is not yet a literature review that aims to reconcile them. It is possible that the pulls in opposite directions help to maintain some balance within the colony, and it is also likely that some of these factors are only acting on some members of the colony and not the entire population.

With those caveats in place, let’s look at some of the factors that impact JH levels.

Temperature and CO2 Levels

A group of researchers in 1983 found that when temperature and CO2 conditions match ideal brood-rearing conditions, more juvenile hormone is produced. They suggest that this would only impact bees on brood combs and not bees that spend their time on food frames at the periphery of the nest. They also noted that their lab study did not perfectly mimic the way that bees can move between frames and so their results might be stronger than what would be seen in real life. Nevertheless, they believe that this impact on JH facilitates the transition of more bees to foraging, to support the brood (Buhler, Lanzrein, and Wille, 1983).

Brood Pheromone

A 2001 study found that the presence of brood pheromone decreases juvenile hormone in nurse bees. This effectively ties nurse bees to their jobs by delaying their transition to outdoor jobs. This relationship feels very intuitive, but the researchers admit that the link is not a strong one and that brood pheromone might have a primer effect (indirect and slower acting), and not a releaser effect (direct and faster acting) (Le Conte, Mohammedi, Robinson, 2001).

Queen Pheromone

In 1992, another group of researchers found that the presence of a mated queen decreases juvenile hormone. Again, this is intuitive because as it could maintain more bees as nurses to care for the brood the queen is producing. In the absence of queen pheromone, JH levels rise. The presence of queen pheromone was found to have a primer effect similar to the presence of brood pheromone, but the researchers suggest that the relationship between queen pheromone and JH could be important to maintaining balance within the colony (Kaatz, Hildebrandt, and Engels, 1992).

Brood and queen pheromone would both be present in abundance in a queenright colony, lowering levels of juvenile hormone in young bees who move around the hive in closest proximity to the brood and queen. This would help to tie them to their essential roles inside the hive. However, in hopelessly queenless colonies, JH levels could rise, leading bees to become more defensive (more responsive to alarm pheromone) and more likely to leave the hive to guard and/or forage. The impact of temperature and CO2 has the opposite effect on JH, lowering it when brood conditions are not maintained. This might help maintain some kind of fragile balance in queenless hives.

Pesticides

Certain neonicotinoids can cause JH to spike, effectively rushing the transition from nurse bee to forager. The researchers who conducted this study in 2021 also found that the unnatural levels of juvenile hormone caused by exposure to neonicotinoids can cause problems with caste differentiation and queen quality (Honghong et al., 2021).

While most neonicotinoids are advertised as relatively safer for bees, it is these indirect impacts that could cause havoc on the beekeeping industry in ways that are difficult to link directly back to the pesticides themselves.

Ongoing Research

There is still a lot that we don’t know about juvenile hormone and its unique role in honey bees, including how external factors might influence colony behaviour and physiology, by impacting JH levels. Questions for future research include: how JH influences bees’ ability to be flexible in their division of labour, what role JH might have in visual/spatial learning that could help explain its link with foraging and flight, and what impact other pesticides can have on queen quality and worker behaviour due to their interaction with juvenile hormone.

In summary, juvenile hormone induces essential changes in honey bees, both in their larval development on later in adult life. Various conditions, both internal to the hive and external, as well as both natural and human-made, act on juvenile hormone levels in both stages of life and can induce behavioural and physiological changes. This continues to be an important and exciting area of honey bee research.

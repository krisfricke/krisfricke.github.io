Swarming Impulse

Bruce Ward · Pages 21–23 · October 2025

Social insects usually start new colonies in one of two ways:

A single fertilised queen starts a new colony on her own. This is the strategy used by many social wasps and ants.

An existing colony splits into two or more parts with the new colony starting off with a viable queen and a good number of workers move to a new site and form a new colony. This is the strategy used by our honey bees and the process is known as swarming.

The swarming process in honey bees is quite simple at the most basic level. The parent hive starts to prepare a batch of new queens. These new queens take 16 days to hatch. Before the new queens start to hatch, the old queen and a proportion of the workers leave the hive and fly off to find a new home. One of the new queens will mate and take over the laying duties in the parent hive. (Bees may also swarm if their hive becomes uninhabitable. These ‘absconding’ swarms are very different and not discussed further in this article.)

Swarming behaviour is not a strategic activity planned and directed by the queen. It is the result of thousands of instinctive decisions made by the individual bees in the hive. Those decisions are driven by seasonal and chemical signals which combine into a collective ‘impulse’ to swarm.

Critical signals that can contribute to the swarming impulse include:

  • Overcrowding and congestion inside the hive.
  • Supplies of fresh pollen and nectar.
  • A lack of cells available for the queen to lay.
  • The presence of drones in the hive.
  • Reducing levels of queen pheromone.
  • Reducing levels of brood pheromone.
  • Increasing daylength.

Some of these are stronger drivers of swarm behaviour than others and different breeds of bees are likely to respond differently to the signals.

Fresh pollen and nectar are an essential precursor, but a heavy honey flow will usually suppress swarming. It also makes sense that hives will only swarm when drones are present.

Overcrowding is one of the strongest drivers if the swarming impulse. It is not the total strength of the hive, but the ratio of bees to hive space, and the ratio of nurse bees to field workers that is important. All hives will be likely to swarm if the population exceeds the available space. But hives living in small spaces are more likely to run out of space than hives living in large spaces.

A lack of space for the queen to lay is also a powerful swarm trigger. A two-deck hive in spring will need at least seven full-depth frames of brood. If the brood nest is constrained by a queen excluder, and some of the brood frames are filled with honey, nectar and pollen, then there will not be enough space for a good queen to lay at her full capacity. If the bees are collecting good quantities nectar, they will use empty brood cells as temporary nectar storage. While this nectar is usually moved further into the hive during the night, this temporary storage can further reduce the cells available for the queen to lay.

Queen and brood pheromones play an important role in hive stability. Queen pheromones decline slowly as the queen ages. Hives with older queens are more likely to swarm than hives with younger queens. Brood pheromones will reduce if the brood area is restricted. Queen and brood pheromones also become increasingly diluted in overcrowded hives.

Changing daylength is a trigger for many biological processes in plants and animals. Hives are more inclined to swarm in spring than at any other time, but if all the stronger triggers are in place, hives can swarm at other times of the year.

The swarming impulse is likely triggered well before we as beekeepers can see any signs. It is fair to assume that if it is spring and the bees have started rearing drones, then at least some of the basic swarm impulse triggers are in place.

The first sign the beekeeper is likely to see is the production of fresh ‘queen cups’ in the hive. There will almost always be queen cups in the hive throughout the year, but the bees will make new ones, usually around the bottom edges of the brood nest. These cups are just the first step, a sign that at least some of the triggers are in place. The cups will remain empty until the hive commits itself to the swarming process. The presence of fresh cell cups is not a sign that the hive is preparing to swarm, just that many of the basic swarming triggers are in place. You would expect to see fresh cell cups in most healthy hives during the swarming season.

The next sign is when the queen cups contain eggs or larvae. This is a sign that the hive is actually preparing to swarm. The swarm clock starts ticking when the first egg is laid in a queen cell cup. The swarm will leave between 8 and 14 days after this first egg is laid.

By the time eggs appear in the queen cups, the workers will have already started feeding the queen a reduced diet. This dramatically reduces her egg laying and over the next week, she loses 30–50% of her weight and shrinks in size, so she can fly when the swarm departs.

The first queen cell will be capped after eight days. The hive will have reduced foraging activity and scout bees will be scouring the neighbourhood for potential sites for a new hive. Other workers will be starting to gorge on honey. The hive is primed and ready to swarm. Now they wait for the right day. Swarms can depart as soon as the first cells are sealed, but they might delay for up to six days.

Finally the swarm will leave the hive, with their old queen. They will fly a short distance and collect together in a cluster while the scout bees finalise their choice of a new home. This swarm is called the prime swarm.

In some seasons, additional swarms will leave with virgin (unmated) queens. These swarms are much smaller and are called ‘cast’ or secondary swarms. In less favourable seasons, the first virgin queen to emerge will tear down the remaining queen cells. If two virgin queens meet, they will fight to the death.

After the last swarm has left, a remaining virgin queen will mate and take over as the laying queen. The worker population of the parent hive will be replenished as the sealed brood emerges, but it will take many weeks to recover the full population.

Beekeeping tips

Swarming is one of the big challenges for beekeepers. While many of us got our first hive by collecting a swarm, it is not good beekeeping practice to allow our hives to swarm, particularly if we live in an urban area.

The way to limit swarming is to address the primary causes of the swarming impulse. While the seasonal influence and increasing daylight are out of our control, we can avoid overcrowding, make sure the queen has room to lay and try to maintain young queens in our hives.

Most importantly, we have to manage our hives more intensively during the swarming season, checking that they have space and looking for the signs that they are preparing to swarm. You need to inspect your hives thoroughly every week or ten days. If the swarming pressure is high, ten days is too long – it can take just eight days to go from the first eggs being laid in swarm cells until the swarm departs. So weekly checks are the best bet.

Keep checking that the bees have enough space and the queen has room to lay. There should be some empty combs in the brood nest area. A two-deck hive with a young queen laying at full capacity needs at least seven full-depth frames available in the brood nest.

Destroying queen cells is not a reliable way of preventing swarming. If there are sealed queen cells in the hive, the hive can swarm at any time. I have seen a hive swarm shortly after a beekeeper had destroyed all the queen cells. I have also seen a lot of inexperienced beekeepers destroy queen cells after the hive had swarmed. More often, the beekeeper fails to see one of the cells, and Murphey’s Law says that will be the smallest cell, likely to produce an inferior queen.

If you find swarm cells in your hive, the only reliable way to stop the swarm is to split the hive. If you can find the queen, you can make an artificial swarm. Move the hive to one side – it doesn’t have to be far. Even turning it to face the other way is enough. Put a new brood box with combs or foundation onto the old hive site and put the queen and bees off several frames into the new box. You can put some brood into this hive, but that is not essential. If you do add brood, make extra sure there are no queen cells. Most of the field bees will join the queen in the new box because it is on the old site. This is your ‘artificial’ swarm. This hive will have a higher proportion of field bees over nurse bees, lowered levels of brood pheromone, and plenty of space for the queen to lay so most of the strongest drivers of the swarming impulse have been removed.

Leave most of the bees and brood in the old hive. Destroy all but the largest queen cell. The absence of queen pheromone in this hive will trick the bees into believing that the hive has swarmed, and with only one queen cell left, they will not be tempted to send out a second swarm with the virgin queen. Leave this hive alone for two or three weeks before checking that the new queen is laying.

‘But I don’t want more hives!’ You can recombine the hives after the swarming season is over, or you can sell or give away the extra hive.

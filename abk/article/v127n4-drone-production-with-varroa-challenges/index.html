Drone Production with Varroa challenges

Liz Frost · Pages 32–33 · October 2025

Ensuring quality queen mating is more important than ever where varroa is established. Varroa is a significant new stress to drone production and quality queen mating. Whether you’re a large-scale queen breeder or a beekeeper who annually makes a few “walk-away” splits, not purposely grafting and raising queen cells, the impact of varroa on the drone supply around your hives is something you’ll need to proactively manage once varroa is established. Where varroa is established in Western Sydney, drone numbers have been decimated.

Michael Duncan has monitored five drone congregation sites around Western Sydney University’s Richmond NSW campus over the past decade. A drone congregation area is where both drones and queens fly to mate when they’re sexually mature. In Australia, mating flights typically happen between 10:00am and 5:00pm, during clear weather, temperatures over 20°C, and wind speeds of less than 19 km per hour. Drone ballooning may capture drones representing hives from within a 44km2 area.

Drone numbers caught at Richmond drone congregation areas between 2015 to 2025 have changed dramatically since varroa established in the area. The results from this year (March 2025) and the previous decade are:

Two Australian studies suggest an average of 28 drone mates per virgin queen is the norm. Based on a 28 drone mating, the Western Sydney Uni 500+ drone catch would only be sufficient to mate 17 virgin queens. The 2025 drone catch at Western Sydney Uni wasn’t enough to even mate one queen sufficiently.

The following dot points are causes and effects of poorly mated queens.

Poorly mated queens:

  • Not enough drones available to mate with
  • Available drones too closely related to the queen
  • Poor brood viability: brood pattern visibly patchy
  • Early supersedure: runs out of stored sperm & culled by workers
  • Lower egg-laying capacity = lower overall hive productivity
  • Hive more susceptible to environmental, pest, disease and nutritional stressors

Additionally, it’s been shown that well-mated queens produce hives that typically outperform poorly mated queens in honey production and are more resilient to stressors.

When beekeepers need to actively produce and retain high numbers of drones, Varroa reproduction increases and may result in more frequent mite treatment. On average, Varroa produce 1.4 mature females when they reproduce in worker brood versus 2.4 mature females in drone brood (Fig 1). Each Varroa may complete 1-3 reproductive cycles.

Recipe for ample drones and well-mated queens:

  • Introduce drone comb into “drone mother” hives at least 40 days before you need mature, adult drones
  • Produce a minimum of 28 drones per queen. (Aim for 30+ drones per queen as not all drones make it to sexual maturity)
  • Ensure a continuous supply of incoming, fresh pollen and light nectar flow. If this isn’t available, provide supplements or move to better conditions
  • Maintain varroa levels all season around the thresholds recommended in the National Varroa Mite Management Program (website: varroa.org.au).

Take this winter to refresh your knowledge on drone production (aka “drone mother”) management from my AgGuide: Queen bee breeding (currently in revision!) or drone guru Dr. John Rhodes’ factsheet “Drone honey bees – rearing and maintenance.” John studied Australian drone quality during his PhD. He stressed the following:

The four basic requirements of drone rearing and maintenance:

  • The provision of surplus quality pollen at all times is essential to produce adequate numbers of mature drones, as is sufficient sugar syrup or honey stores. The pollen may be either a natural pollen or a protein supplement to complement the pollen available from the environment (quality and quantity)
  • Use strong colonies headed by a proven queen preferably more than one year old
  • Drones are produced earlier if a drone comb is placed in the middle of the brood chamber in autumn, preferably a comb with a mixture of drone and worker cells. In spring add one or two frames containing drone cells as hive strength and weather conditions allow
  • Drone mother colonies require the same amount of management as queen cell raising colonies.

An additional requirement with varroa will be to monitor your mite levels monthly and treat as necessary to keep your levels at or below threshold. Look into all available Varroa mite treatments, which ones are effective during the breeding season and how they may affect drone rearing. Formic Pro for example may have adverse effects on open brood, hatching bees, and cause workers to remove drone eggs, thus delaying drone production, as noted by the National Varroa Mite Management Program (varroa.org.au).

Drone mother stocking rate

  • Stocking rate is based on the following:
  • Virgin queens mate with an average of 28 drones
  • One full-depth drone comb may yield 3,500 mature drones in perfect conditions (when does this ever happen?)
  • Environmental conditions vary (pollen, nectar availability)
  • Absence of feral drones
  • Presence of other managed drones with undesirable genetics.

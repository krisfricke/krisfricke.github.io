Supers to the side!

Lynne Swanson · Pages 13–15 · October 2025

A new horizontal hive for hobby beekeepers

Hobby beekeepers have lots of options when it comes to choosing hive configuration. When I looked into getting my first hives, I looked at the pluses and minuses of five different options from two different perspectives. First, from that of a 163cm, middle-aged woman (me), and second, from the bees themselves.

Full disclosure: my background is that of a retired veterinarian with an interest in animal behavior, so I know the stress that can ensue unnecessarily when our approach to different species reflects only human culture and not that of an animal (or, in this case, an insect).

The classes I attended through my local association emphasized the use of vertical Langstroth hives in eight- and ten-frame boxes. This reflects many years of tradition and this hive’s adaptability to commercial beekeeping situations. But Langstroth boxes necessitate heavy lifting that I need to avoid due to back and strength issues. Also, I am never going to be a commercial beekeeper.

Five-frame nucleus hives cut the weight of these boxes down, but colonies in these hives build up fast, with less room for expansion. I found their strength was in being used as emergency hives and swarm traps.

Because I love the idea of having everything I need at a reasonable working height - as opposed to leaning over to pick things up - I was intrigued by the idea of horizontal hives. But what kind of horizontal hive to use?

I ruled out using top bar hives because I wanted full frames for honey harvesting.

I liked the idea of Long Langs, but their depth of only one deep frame is quite limiting when bees are storing winter resources. I’ve since built two long Langs, but I use them primarily for spring nuc propagation.

Layens hives, with their deep and narrow, 33x40.6cm frames, give bees lots of extra room for brood and resources. They’re nicely insulated against weather extremes (the most extreme, for me, being the heat of a North Carolina summer), and the bees love them – just ask anyone who has these hives – but Layens frames don’t fit into standard honey extractors, and a Layens extractor is pretty pricey. I just couldn’t get around that, budget-wise. I also saw the value of bee space above my frames. Layens frames don’t allow for this.

Since I wasn’t finding the hive of my dreams, I pulled out the drawing board. The “Supers-to-the-Side” (STS) hive was the result. It’s an insulated, horizontal, two-colony hive with mega-frames for brood, standard Langstroth frames for honey and perks that make observation of my hives and integrated pest management (IPM) easier. Awesome!

I couldn’t believe no one had come up with this design. Bits and pieces of it, yes, but not the exact same hive. The closest I found was a hive Jean Herpin designed in the mid-1900s. It had large frames for brood and standard frames for honey. But there was no flexibility in the size of the brood and honey areas; it wasn’t insulated like a bee tree, and it held only one colony. My own design solved these problems.

Fast forward 20 months, since an idea is just that – an idea. Its proof is in how it holds up in the real world after testing, troubleshooting and perfecting. After building six two-colony STS hives, installing two packages, four splits, one feral cut-out and five swarms, putting everything through seven full seasons of use and making a few modifications, measuring both hive temperatures and humidity throughout the year and comparing the performance of these hives to three other styles of hive in the same apiary: oh my goodness, I love these hives. The bees do well in them, too, and I’m thoroughly enjoying the honey they’re producing.

Here are 11 reasons why the Supers-to-the-Side horizontal hive has become our apiary’s favorite and we’re transitioning all of our bee colonies to this style of hive:

  1. The STS hive is very user-friendly. As with other horizontal hives, it is easy to uncover the part of a colony you wish to view without upsetting the entire colony.
  2. Like other long hives, this one is kind to your back and your knees. No leaning down. No heavy lifting. Everything you need is at tabletop height, with lots of room to rest things. Your hive tools. Extra frames. Packages of new bees. Items you don’t want to lay on the ground.
  3. The mega-frames used for brood and resources are easy to maneuver, allowing you to view the actions of more bees at a time. A mega-frame is the size of one Langstroth deep plus a medium plus an inch (gaining an extra inch of comb space where a top and bottom bar have been removed). A frame full of brood weighs just 1.8-2.7kg, and a frame half full of capped honey going into the winter weighs 3.6-4kg, not unreasonable at all. They are built using standard Langstroth tops and bottoms, and you can purchase the 40cm side pieces from Little Creek Farms at https://lc-farms.com.
  4. Like 33x40cm Layens frames, 23x40cm mega-frames allow lots of important winter resources to be stored above the brood. Top bar and 23cm deep long Langs just don’t offer this kind of room above winter brood.
  5. When it comes to honey frames, this hive utilizes standard deep and medium frames, so special extractors aren’t required. After cutting back the shoulders of my medium frames, I attach them to the bottoms of deep frames using stainless steel zip ties. I then add the combined frames to hives that have a natural honey bridge (a full frame of broodless honeycomb that works like a queen excluder). At 22 cents apiece, these ties are easy to remove when it is time to harvest honey.
  6. The STS hive incorporates some very convenient features, including IPM pans to trap small hive beetles, wax moth larva and falling mites; ports that make delivering oxalic acid vapor a breeze; bee escapes under the roof (so lingering bees can find their way out), and see-through inner covers with a hole for rapid round feeders and fondant. I love my see-through inner covers! On any but the coldest of winter days - that is, any time the ambient temperatures are over 13 degrees C - I can check bee numbers, activity and food consumption without chilling the bees or breaking propolis seals. Using inexpensive Govee sensors, I can check hive temperature and humidity with just a glance. Placed under each observation window, these 5x7.5cm sensors are completely ignored by the bees, and surprisingly, propolis has never been an issue. When working with one colony, I place a hand towel over the inner cover of the other colony, so as not to disturb them. It works well.
  7. This hive’s insulation (which runs from R-7 on the sides to R-10 on top) keeps a lot of the heat out in the truly hot summer, and it helps to keep the heat of the bees in during the colder months, more similar to a bee tree than a standard vertical hive with an R rating of 0.75. In the high heat of August, temperatures in my STS hives are 3-4 degrees cooler than my other hives. On the colder days of January, temperatures in these insulated hives are a full 6-11 degrees warmer than the other hives, and my bees don’t have to eat as much to maintain their temperature.
  8. The insulation (and reduced honey consumption) also helps to keep winter humidity down, as it would for any hive design. For example, on December 24, 2023, my mostly uninsultated Langstroth hives had readings of 85%, 77%, 71% and 88% humidity, and condensation could be seen around the top edges of two of the hives. Contrast that with readings of 55%, 56% and 62% in the insulated STS hives. Adequate top insulation in particular means no cold surface above the bees to collect condensation.
  9. These hives are stable. We are in a hurricane-prone area with plenty of black bears. In both cases, it is the vertical hives we worry about first. STS hives do well in the wind, and while their stability doesn’t make them bear-proof, it can’t hurt should our local bears come closer to our apiary than they have to date.
  10. Since the follower boards used in horizontal hives allow for examination and manipulation of bee colonies with less interruption than in vertical hives, and since mega-frames give bees lots of room to move around while they’re being inspected, I truly believe the bees in these hives are calmer. I don’t need to use a smoker, a point that is important to me because I have asthma.
  11. Lastly, since they are built using commonsense layers, first from the bottom up, and then from the bee cavity out, it doesn’t take specialized woodworking knowledge to build a Supers-to-the-Side hive, simply attention to detail and the ability to measure and cut materials accurately. A two-colony hive can be built using just $500 worth of materials. Two videos detail how, and a tools and materials list is included: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TsJHjOBE0Eg https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LGz22X-8UlQ

Lynne Swanson is a retired veterinarian with particular interests in canine and honey bee behavior. She volunteers at Safe Harbor Farm’s teaching apiary in coastal North Carolina, U.S. Her YouTube channel for dog and honey bee enthusiasts includes videos showing her working STS hives. The channel is givesmiles.us.

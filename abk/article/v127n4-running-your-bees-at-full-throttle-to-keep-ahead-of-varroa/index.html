Running Your Bees at Full Throttle to Keep Ahead of Varroa

Rod Bourke · Pages 36–38 · October 2025

As varroa becomes more established there will be a lot of opportunity for beekeepers, at least for those beekeepers that still have live bee hives. Unfortunately, there will be a proportion of our current beekeeping community that will not be able to keep their hives alive. They will eventually either realise that they need to greatly improve themselves to stay on in the industry, or that it is all getting too hard and that beekeeping is no longer viable for them. Some do not realise this, but after losing all their hives they simply cannot afford to buy more hives and then never restart. Others will restart but may never be able to become viable.

For decades before varroa arrived too many beekeepers have been running far more hives that they can properly manage, but the luck that enabled them to just get by dries up once varroa mite is added to the beekeeping equation. The good old days are over, so it is time to forget them and get on with the extra work we must now do to keep our bees healthy and productive. Wherever varroa has arrived in the world it has always been very hard on the type of operator that runs more hives than they should, as it exposes and puts additional pressure on all the other poor beekeeping practices that were already being undertaken.

Whatever the case, if your beekeeping operation is suffering from unmanaged varroa and other bee pests and diseases then your bees are not performing optimally, and your operation is probably minimally or not at all profitable. To run your beekeeping operation successfully you need to have good healthy bees, so if you are not achieving this and varroa is taking them down then your operation is currently failing.

During the period that those hives are failing (but still existing on apiary sites) they will be a major biosecurity risk to every other beehive around them. There are two really good ways that this biosecurity and financial catastrophe can be avoided, either by selling up before this stage is reached or by taking the bull by the horns and going hard at becoming a better beekeeper.

Leaving the industry can be a very hard decision to make but one that’s made easier with a path to follow: start considering the following:

  • How many hives of good quality do you have left
  • How many weak hives do you have that could be united to make viable colonies, or pulled out
  • How many diseased hives need to be pulled out and burned/irradiated
  • How many Public Lands sites (paid up or transferrable) do you have that are valuable and give security to a buyer
  • How many viable private sites do you have that a buyer may have some interest in (remembering that such sites are often not very secure and can be more easily lost)
  • How much remaining value does your plant, excess bee equipment and any trucks or other machinery have.

Often sellers want a far higher price than everything is worth to a buyer in a current market, so in a time like now when honey prices are low and varroa is rampaging through the countryside you do need to be realistic about how much everything is worth. The sale price of your hives may not be much at all, but when you consider that you were experiencing high hive losses, then getting something for your remaining hives is better than nothing. Don’t fall into that trap of saying that you cannot afford to treat…if you spend $10 per hive on one treatment and then sell the hive for even $200-250 (or got 2-3 boxes of honey off it) then you got a lot more for it than if you skipped the treatment and just let it die.

A struggling beekeeper selling their bees and getting out is going to save other beekeepers around them much anguish if those failing colonies (that are becoming mite and AFB bombs) become managed ones by someone else. Cattle and sheep get bought and sold every day when forage and seasonal conditions dictate, and it should be exactly the same for beehives if high numbers cannot be adequately supported by their current owner.

Upskilling

If you know that you need assistance or have done a poor job managing varroa and acknowledge that you need to do better than you have then there are many pathways you can take to improve yourself as a beekeeper.

  1. Contact the Varroa Development Officers to get them to visit and assist you to manage your current varroa situation. - www.varroa.org.au
  2. If you also have other pest or disease issues then ask your VDO if they can bring a BBO along too, as they can offer ongoing assistance to enable you to clean up your American foulbrood (AFB) and other issues that are affecting your hive health.
  3. Do varroa training, in person or online via www.varroa.org.au
  4. Bee Biosecurity (Bee Pest and Disease) training is also available through Tocal College and BBOs if your state currently has them.
  5. Align yourself with another operator that is managing their varroa well and learn some tricks from them. A good mentor is a valuable resource so you may find it best to do some work with them to really learn how they operate.
  6. Prepare yourself to try, learn and do new things. Just because you did beekeeping a particular way for a long time does not mean that is the right way to continue operating now. Varroa mite changes everything, so you need to change your beekeeping to respond to that changing situation.
  7. Look at your operation and work out what is generating income for you and what is a realistic number of hives to run. A lot of beekeepers fall into the trap of thinking more hives make more money, but the reality is that is completely wrong. You need to look at how much income each hive generates per year, and to be truly successful that should be a high number.
  8. Run fewer but high-quality hives. You may factor in that every one of your hives are going to do an almond and/or other pollination job, plus make a lot of honey. The reality is that if you have many weak, poorly managed low-quality colonies then they will either get rejected from pollination, or a smaller percentage of them be paid for. Either way, inferior quality hives can cost you a lot of money (pollination that you were not paid for), plus wasted spots on a truck where better hives could have been. Transporting non-paying hives (dead wood) to a honey flow or pollination job means a higher transport cost is allocated to every real hive in the load, which is just plain poor business.

If every one of the hives on your truck is up to scratch, then you have minimised the transport cost of each hive to its lowest possible amount, increasing the profit margin that you gain from the pollination fee or honey production for each hive. Profit enables miticides, fuel, truck services and wages to be paid, that keep the operation running smoothly.

By running 300-400 awesome colonies per qualified beekeeper instead of a higher number of weaker colonies (that perform poorly) then you will have far more time to manage each hive, reduce swarming (that reduces feral mite bombs), achieve significantly higher honey yields per colony (because each hive is higher quality) and outlay lower costs for miticides too (as you are not wasting dollars treating dead-wood colonies that provide you with nothing in return).

It is now time to re-evaluate your operation and determine how many less hives you may need to run per beekeeper to keep in front of varroa, or how many more beekeepers you may need to put on to manage your current numbers appropriately. This may also be a great opportunity for you to cull out any AFB hives that you have been propping up with antibiotics, so if you would like assistance managing your current internal AFB problem (with the eventual aim to run a clean operation without the regular use of antibiotics) then send an email to bee.biosecurity@dpi.nsw.gov.au or call me on 0438 677 195.

The BBO (Bee Biosecurity Officer) team regularly assists beekeepers in this regard, as beekeeping without AFB is a far better way to operate, instead of masking it and allowing it to continue spreading further within your operation and at the same time devaluing it at a future time when you may want to sell. With varroa now taking its own toll on your bees who needs to also be propping up a heap of diseased hives that will cause a whole lot more problems if varroa weakens them and they get robbed out. Time to run your bees at full throttle to keep ahead of varroa.

Rod Bourke hails from a commercial beekeeping family with 4000 hives in Tasmania. In addition to working for many years in the family business, Rod has also been a prawn, coral, giant clam and seahorse farmer. He has been a Bee Biosecurity Officer in NSW since 2017

AHBIC Chair & CEO Reports

AHBIC · Page 35 · August 2026

Over the last couple of months, AHBIC has been attending the state conferences, culminating in Tasmania followed by the AHBIC AGM. All state conferences have been well attended this year, demonstrating the continued thirst for knowledge as we navigate the challenges of managing varroa. Most conferences have had a strong mix of domestic and international speakers and extensive trade displays.

As we move through winter and beekeepers begin to open hives in preparation for spring and pollination events, we are receiving mixed reports on hive strength and numbers. Varroa and the impacts of the resistant varroa population continue to significantly influence hive numbers, with many beekeepers reporting large colony losses. Unfortunately, current jurisdictional data systems are not timely enough to capture these colony losses in real-time, making the Colony Loss Survey even more important this year. The survey is due to open in September/October, and it is more important than ever to participate because this data influences government decisions.

2025/2026 Season

The 2025/2026 season has been one of the toughest periods Australian beekeepers have witnessed. Historically we referenced weather patterns, prospects and production, today the focus is on varroa and business viability.

The discovery of a new incursion of varroa that is resistant to the two main chemical classes has come as a shock for industry. Our thoughts are with those operators who learnt they were dealing with the resistant strain too late. This was not operator error, rather being caught off guard by something that we did not imagine occurring for decades. Now that the beekeeping community understands and recognises that we are dealing with a different beast, we as beekeepers can adapt and adopt alternative treatment methods moving forward.

With any crisis comes opportunity. For the willing, the future is bright. Several reports recently published explain that Australia will be short of bees for pollination events in the foreseeable future. Less bees will result in less honey being produced, which will result in Australian honey becoming a premium. Australian beekeepers are renowned for their resilience and adaptability. Those who can combine the craft of successful beekeeping in Australia with varroa management will prosper.

Honey Bee Industry Emergency Roundtable

The current varroa situation facing our industry does not fit within any pre-existing government framework that allows governments to easily activate industry support. We are no longer in the biosecurity emergency response framework, as we have transitioned to management, and varroa is not considered a natural disaster. As a result, we are seeing governments struggle to find ways to support industry. To complicate the situation, beekeepers are mobilising, sending letters to ministers, speaking in the media and making representations to local MPs, all of which is raising the profile and importance of the challenge. However, inconsistencies in

messaging and in what we are asking of government have slowed their response.

The AHBIC team are very much in touch with the beekeepers on the ground. Across the AHBIC staff and executive, there is a constant stream of communication that results in AHBIC having a true understanding of what is happening. Behind the scenes, the AHBIC team have constant discussions on the impacts that beekeepers are experiencing using this information to act. AHBIC is listening. The challenge is determining how to help industry and what to advocate for that is realistic. This is a common challenge for any agricultural representative body. To be taken seriously, AHBIC must go to government with a clear, well thought out, achievable request.

To provide greater clarity to governments, AHBIC, in collaboration with the Queensland Beekeepers Association and the Queensland Government, has held a Honey Bee Industry Emergency Roundtable. The aim of the roundtable is not to hold a talkfest, but to run an action-oriented process that first agrees on a set of prioritised, deliverable actions and then present those actions to governments seeking agreement.

The first of the two meetings, the technical workshop held on 30 June, brought together 40 representatives from industry, governments, RDCs and pollination-dependent sectors, with half of the participants being beekeepers. A total of 55 actions gathered from industry across 10 themes were considered by the workshop. Participants worked through each action, ensuring it was deliverable, impactful and feasible under government policies and regulations. They then prioritised the themes and actions.

These 55 actions were then distilled into 11 actions based on the technical workshop prioritisation and feedback. The 11 actions have been presented to all levels of government, seeking agreement at a Decision Makers Forum on 28 July. This forum includes representatives from each jurisdiction at the most senior levels, ensuring decisions can be made.

While unconventional, this approach provides a clear set of agreed actions, strengthens the case for support and will identify any barriers to agreement. The post-forum, industry-facing paper will provide beekeepers with a clear lobbying document they can use to ensure consistency in messaging.

Imported honey

AHBIC's consistent work in this area over the past four years has finally shown results. DAFF have been very accommodating to work with, despite being painfully slow! In late 2025 and early 2026 DAFF engaged the states and territories food authorities to conduct a spontaneous survey, to collect samples from importers. Under the current protocol in the Imported Food Inspection Scheme (IFIS), all samples passed for adulteration. However, when the same samples were tested via NMR testing, a significant problem was discovered.

This clearly demonstrates that the current protocol is insufficient. DAFF are currently investigating the next course of action. The objective for AHBIC is to have the IFIS protocol adjusted to a modern-day method. This would be a huge win for the Australian beekeeping industry.

The roll of volunteers

I would like to take the opportunity to thank all the people who volunteer their time for the benefit of the industry. There are likely over a hundred people who contribute to an organization in one way or another. Whether it be cooking the barbeque at your local branch, working at shows, sitting on committees or executives, it all helps and contributes to the common goal to better our industry.

It is important to be supportive of each other and encourage people to step up and contribute to these roles. Social media highlights everything that we do, it is important not to criticize individuals online, particularly for holding a position where they are volunteering. For the thick skinned, it may be water off a ducks back, however the next generation are watching. In the back of their minds, they may be hesitant to step up observing this criticism. Think about what you say, is it necessary or helpful?

Australian Honey Bee Industry Council Annual General Meeting

On Sunday, 12 July, AHBIC held its AGM at the Launceston Country Club. The AGM attracted 25 observers and 20 representatives from around the country. In addition to the standard procedural content, delegates heard from Dr John Roberts from CSIRO about Deformed Wing Virus (DWV) and the ability of drone semen and imported honey to carry viable DWV. Delegates passed a series of motions for AHBIC to lobby the federal government to ensure the risks are appropriately managed.

Participants also heard from ICS Consulting, who recently completed a traceability feasibility project for the federal government through AgriFutures. This project highlighted the possibility of a national hive traceability program that could track hives for biosecurity and honey traceability purposes. Delegates gained insights into the proposed structure and design of the potential system. They also heard about the strong government interest in the system, while gaining a clear understanding of the many barriers that would need to be overcome.

Chair and executive elections were held with Jon Lockwood being re-appointed for a two-year term along with Dr Cooper Schouten also being re-appointed for a second two-year term. We welcomed Mick Palmer from Tasmania who was voted in to replace the retiring Dr Ben McKee who has served his maximum 3 continuous terms.

I would like to acknowledge Dr Ben McKee's tenure on AHBIC. Not only has Ben served three terms as a director he has also served as Chair of AHBIC's Food Safety Committee and as a delegate for the Honey Packers and Marketers Association for many years.

Australian Pesticides and Veterinary Medicines Authority (APVMA)

AHBIC, in collaboration with the NSW Apiarists' Association and representatives from Amateur Beekeepers Australia, held a meeting with APVMA executives to discuss issues facing the industry and gain a deeper understanding of APVMA's processes. The meeting covered the current challenges with label and permit inconsistencies and limitations, own-use permits, and the costs involved in treating for varroa. We also discussed providing beekeepers with additional treatment options and how we can attract international manufacturers to the Australian market.

The Month Ahead

Over the next month, AHBIC will be busy producing the industry-facing summary of the roundtable and following up on any actions agreed to in principle at the forum. We will also hold an AHBIC Executive meeting, a Producer Contingency Fund meeting and Australian Colony Loss Survey Steering Committee meetings.

We continue to press government on imported honey and, once the DWV-in-honey report is published, we will use it to seek additional biosecurity conditions on imported honey. A great deal of work on levy reform has also been happening behind the scenes, with the industry-facing campaign getting closer to launch.

A number of motions from the AHBIC AGM will require immediate action, and we will work on these over the coming month.

For those participating in upcoming pollination events, I hope you travel safely, your bees are in the condition you expect, and we all have a productive start to the 2026-27 honey season.

Honey bees' sense of smell changes from larval to adult life stages, study finds The change is thought to be the result of social evolution UNIVERSITY OF ILLINOIS AT URBANA-CHAMPAIGN, NEWS BUREAU Honey bee larvae lack the sophisticated olfactory capabilities of adult honey bees, a new study finds. Scientists point to this temporary loss-of-function as a side effect of the nurse bees' heroic level of brood care, calling it a consequence of social evolution. The new findings are detailed in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. Honey bees are highly eusocial, an evolutionarily advanced system in which reproduction is limited to one or a very few individuals while nonreproductive workers care for the young. Various bee species differ in the amount of attention they bestow on their young, with honey bees representing one of the most intensive levels of care. In their short lives, most honey bees go from being helpless larvae — confined to honeycomb cells made of wax and dependent on a horde of nurse bees for their roughly 100 daily feedings and inspections — to adults that take on the various duties required to maintain and protect the colony, said Gene Robinson, an entomologist and the executive director and CEO of the Discovery Partners Institute at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign. Robinson led the new study with U. of I. postdoctoral researcher Tianfei Peng. Because they are so well cared for, honey bee larvae have no need to feed themselves, unlike the larvae of many other insect species. .. Adult honey bee foragers must be able to distinguish odors from a vast number of potential sources of pollen and nectar. For this, they rely on a suite of chemosensory receptors, including olfactory receptors, or ORs, and ionotropic receptors, or IRs. The new study focused on a coreceptor, ORCO, that is essential to OR function; and IR25a, one of several coreceptors needed for proper IR function… In a series of experiments, the team found that gene expression of ORCO in the antennae and brain was reduced in honey bee larvae compared with their adult counterparts.

Insurance and Risk Management for Beekeepers: More Than Just Public Liability

By Tony Wilsmore

For most beekeepers, insurance is something we think about only when renewing memberships, applying for a site permit, or after hearing about someone else's misfortune. Yet as the Australian beekeeping industry becomes more professional, mobile and exposed to increasing risks, insurance and risk management deserve more attention.

Twenty years ago, NSW Agriculture produced an excellent Agnote on insurance for beekeepers, highlighting the importance of public liability, property insurance and personal accident cover. While much has changed since then, the principles remain remarkably relevant. However, the risks facing beekeepers today are broader and more complex.

Public Liability – Your Most Important Protection

Public liability insurance remains the cornerstone of a beekeeper's insurance portfolio. It protects against claims arising from injury or property damage caused by your activities.

Although most of us would consider the likelihood of serious claims to be low, the consequences can be significant. Examples might include:

 A smoker causing a grass or bushfire.  A member of the public being stung after interfer-

ing with hives.

 Bees contributing to a vehicle accident.

 Livestock losses following bee stings.

 Illness caused by contaminated honey or bee products.

Even if liability is ultimately not established, legal costs alone can be substantial.

Many land managers and government agencies require public liability insurance before issuing apiary permits. Historically, $10 million cover was regarded as adequate, but many organisations now require $20 million cover, and commercial operators may wish to consider even higher levels depending on their activities.

Importantly, insurance does not remove the obligation to exercise reasonable care. Good risk management remains the first line of defence.

Does Your Home Insurance Cover Your Bees?

Many hobby beekeepers assume that their household insurance will automatically cover their hives and equipment. Unfortunately, this may not always be the case.

Some policies exclude business activities or have

limits on structures and contents located away from the home. If your bees are located on another property, in a community garden, or on leased land, it is worth checking exactly what is and is not covered.

The first question to ask your insurer is simple: "Are my hives, equipment and activities as a beekeeper covered under my existing policy?"

Protecting Your Investment

Hive theft, bushfires, storms and vandalism are unfortunate realities for many Australian beekeepers.

Commercial operators have long recognised the importance of insuring their assets but increasing hive values mean that even recreational beekeepers may have several thousand dollars invested in bees, boxes and extracting equipment.

Property insurance can cover:

 Hives and bees.

 Honey extraction equipment.

 Storage sheds.

 Trailers.

 Vehicles.

 Honey stocks and packaging supplies.

 Damage caused by fire, storms, theft and malicious acts.

The key principle is to insure assets for their replacement value. Under-insurance can result in reduced claim payments and leave the beekeeper effectively sharing the loss with the insurer.

What About Income Protection?

Many sideline and commercial beekeepers are sole operators. If illness or injury prevents you from lifting boxes, driving trucks or extracting honey, income can quickly disappear.

Personal accident and income protection insurance can provide:

 Weekly payments while unable to work.

 Lump-sum payments for permanent disability.

 Funds to employ assistance during recovery.

As we age, this form of insurance can become more important than insuring the hives themselves.

New Risks Facing Modern Beekeepers

The industry of 2026 looks very different from that of 2002 when the original Agnote article was written.

Vehicle and Trailer Risks

Many beekeepers transport hives over long distances. Heavy trailers, forklifts, loaders and truck-mounted equipment create risks that may not be fully covered by standard vehicle policies.

Operators should check:  Trailer insurance.  Load security requirements.  Forklift and machinery cover.  Transit cover for bees and equipment.  Worker and volunteer insurance.

Varroa and Biosecurity

Varroa has changed the economics of beekeeping. Colony losses, treatment failures and declining productivity have become business realities.

Insurance generally does not cover disease losses, making biosecurity and integrated pest management essential forms of risk management. Prevention remains far cheaper than replacement.

Cyber Risks

Increasing numbers of beekeepers sell online, process electronic payments and maintain customer databases.

Loss of data, scams and cybercrime are now risks faced by businesses of every size. Maintaining secure passwords, backups and fraud awareness is becoming just as important as maintaining equipment.

Risk Management is Better Than Insurance

Insurance transfers financial risk, but many losses are preventable.

Good practices include: Fire Prevention

 Carry water, extinguishers and fire rake.

 Use smokers responsibly.

 Avoid operating during Total Fire Ban days.

 Maintain vehicles and trailers. Security

 Mark hives and equipment.

 Keep records and photographs.

 Use GPS and site inventories.

 Consider trail cameras for remote sites. Food Safety

 Maintain clean registered extraction areas.

 Follow withholding periods for treatments.

 Keep batch records.

 Label products correctly. Personal Safety

 Avoid lifting excessive weights.

 Use hive aids and mechanical equipment.

 Work with another person where possible.

 Carry communication devices when working remote apiaries. Use Specialists

The original NSW Agriculture advice remains sound: don't judge insurance purely on price.

Beekeeping has unique risks, and general insurance policies may contain exclusions that only become apparent when a claim is made.

Associations, clubs and specialist brokers can often help identify appropriate cover and explain the fine print. The VAA also offers an insurance cover designed for amateur beekeepers that may be appropriate for your situation.

Final Thoughts

Beekeeping has always involved risk. Weather, fire, theft, disease and accidents are part of the landscape. While no insurance policy can eliminate those risks, the right cover - combined with sensible risk management - can protect both your livelihood and your peace of mind.

Perhaps the most important question every beekeeper should ask is not: "What does insurance cost?"

But rather: "Could I afford the consequences if something went wrong?"

Author's note: This article builds on principles outlined in NSW Agriculture's Insurance for Beekeepers Agnote (2002), while considering the challenges facing contemporary Australian beekeeping.

Bee Intelligence

Bruce Ward · Page 19 · May 2026

Honey bees display a surprising level of intelligence for tiny insects. Their ability to communicate, organise, and adapt to changing environments has fascinated scientists and beekeepers for generations and

The bee's apparent intelligence is expressed in their complex behaviour. Bees can recognise patterns, solve problems and learn from their experiences. In addition to signs of individual intelligence, the honey bee colony as a whole shows signs of collective intelligence, particularly the ability to make consensus decisions that are in the best interests of the hive as a whole.

There is little doubt that the honey bee's intelligence is enabled by its brain, which has the capacity to process complex information from a variety of senses, solve problems and make decisions. It also has areas dedicated to memory, which allows the bee to learn from its experience. For example, a foraging worker can remember where they found a good supply of nectar. They can then return to the same location and recognise the shape and colour of the good flowers before homing-in on the familiar aroma of the good nectar source.

Other aspects of the bee's intelligence are instinctive. Embedded in the DNA of every bee are hardcoded rules that govern how they respond to different situations that they may encounter. For example, if a worker bee encounters the alarm pheromone, they instinctively switch into defensive mode. The intensity of their defence depends on their strain (breed), but all

honey bees instinctively react with defensive behaviour. In laboratory experiments, worker bees can be easily distracted from learning if they are exposed to alarm pheromone. Instinct overrides other brain process in the presence of the alarm pheromone.

The honey bee hive functions like a superorganism, with each bee acting as a cell in a larger body. Communication within the hive is almost certainly at the heart of collective colony intelligence. Bees communicate with a range of behaviours ranging from simple sharing of pheromones and food, to performing complex dances to pass on learning to worker sisters.

By communicating to other bees what they have learned, bees are extending the memory of their experience to other members of the colony and this is in effect a "colony memory' that extends beyond the life of individual bees.

Communication is also an important factor in how a hive makes collective decisions. Some of these decisions occur only once a year, others occur many times each day. A swarm's choice of a new home is the classic case of collective decision making. Individual scout bees find potential sites and communicate the location by dancing. Other bees follow the directions and return to dance themselves. If a worker had a negative experience at the new site, such as close encounter with a predator, they will stop other workers from dancing directions to that location. When enough bees are dancing the same location, that is a majority vote in favour, and the decision is made.

Collective action is based on many individual decisions. Every field bee makes a daily or even hourly decisions on whether to forage for nectar, pollen or water. These decisions are mostly based on things we cannot see or perceive. It is likely that things like temperature, humidity and the balances of specific hormones and pheromones combine, but the collective result of the individual decisions is the complex behaviour that we see in the hive. For example if humidity in the brood nest started to fall, field workers about to leave on a foraging flight might decide to collect water. While the humidity remains low, more and more foragers would divert to water collection until the humidity level increased.

It is important to note that the intelligence that we measure in bees is directly relevant to their biological needs. This is easier to explain with something that bees don't do as well as their relatives, the social wasps – that is logical problem solving. In laboratory experiments, honey bees are able to learn that food from a blue container is better than food from a red container and that the red container is better than a yellow container. But they are unable to translate that learning to know that the blue container is better than the yellow. This sort of logic does not have a role in the hive. Some social wasps can solve these simple logic problems, which is probably related to their different social behaviour. These wasps have multiple queens in one colony and the queens develop a dominance hierarchy. An individual wasp most likely uses the logic ability in

working out their position in the hierarchy.

Most of the scientific study of intelligence has focussed on worker bees, but it makes sense that drones and queens must also use their intelligence to enhance their specific roles in the hive. Drones are capable of sophisticated learning but it is finely tuned to their specific biological mission – finding and mating with a queen.

There is recent evidence suggesting that the learning and memory capabilities of queens are highly advanced and potentially play a crucial role in long-term colony stability. Queens may be even more intelligent than their worker daughters. This may play some role in longer term colony memory.

With all the evidence of the intelligence of bees, it would be easy to over-play the evidence and draw conclusions that are unlikely to be true. A bee's brain has perhaps a million neurons. Imagine that the bee's brain is a 300-page book with a very good index. The bee can easily find and use information from its brain, which is a marvel of miniaturised processing power. The bee applies a relatively small set of instinctive rules to the information coming from their senses, and their memory, resulting in the complex behaviour that we observe.

But for all the marvels of bee behaviour, they are still working with an insect brain with a very limited capacity and their intelligent behaviour is very closely tied to their life and survival.

A human brain has 86 billion neurons, equivalent to the millions of books in the US Library of Congress. While our brain takes more time to find and process this massive information base, we have much larger capacity for memory and learning, including learning about things that have nothing to do with our survival.

It is possible in laboratory experiments to train worker bees to recognise and distinguish between human faces. The bees are almost certainly using the parts of their brain that recognised flowers, and this pattern recognition works with the patterns of human faces. But it a huge leap to suggest that bees learn to recognise their owners.

I don't believe that bees have any awareness that humans exist. We are just a part of the landscape– something that is there and something that sometimes represents a threat. There is a mythology surrounding bees and beekeeping that I don't subscribe to. To my mind, this is largely a construct of our human brain and our propensity to look for a meaning behind everything that we observe, even when there is no meaning there. Beekeeping implications

In April 2024, some of the world's leading scientists signed the New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness, which concludes that there is a realistic possibility of consciousness in insects, including honey bees. It argues that where this possibility exists, we have an ethical obligation to consider their welfare.

I believe that managing beehives can be ethical and humane if beekeepers prioritise bee welfare. Opinions clearly differ and some of our beekeeping decisions involve complex ethical issues.

Bee Intelligence, cont'd

Most real bee “keepers” (as distinct from bee owners) would agree that their bees deserve their respect and most of us go out of our way to look after the welfare of our bees. This includes taking steps to avoid unnecessarily crushing bees when manipulating hives and working hard to place minimal stress on our bees. When it is necessary to kill bees, such as disease control varroa testing or requeening, we try to avoid unnecessary suffering.

In my mind, it is not ethical for a beekeeper to allow

their hives to die of starvation or neglect, just as much as it is with a pet animal. Similarly, I think that good protective equipment is there to protect the beekeeper, not to enable rougher handling than would otherwise be possible. Bruce has been keeping bees on and off since he was 16. He worked as an apiary officer in NSW for 15 years in the 1970s and 80s, then moved into management roles in the Department of Agriculture for the remainder of his working career. In retirement, he has moved to live in Geelong and has resumed beekeeping as a hobby.

Season Report

By John Edmonds

The weather in the start of April has been cooler than average, causing the bees to begin reducing brood and back filling around the brood to get ready for winter. When looking at the brood nest it is deceptive as the bees have fresh nectar but the hive scales around Geelong region have consistently recorded less weight day by day.

At the end of April, we have enjoyed nice sunny autumn days with temperatures of low to mid 20s and little wind. Where the bees are able to find flowers, they are still breeding and have strong clusters of bees. Some hives have little or no brood and have thrown out drones but other hives in areas with more pollen available still maintain drones. The future predictions are for more of similar weather for the next 10 days.

It is disappointing that varroa has been found around Geelong, as I hoped we would have a longer time before it becomes a problem. The news is that it appears another varroa incursion has happened in NSW and Southern Queensland with varroa resistant to the four synthetic treatments that we are allowed to treat the bees. I am reliably told that in the first few years of varroa infection oxalic acid will not eliminate varroa, just maintain the levels. The problem being reinfection causes huge increases in varroa levels.

Kris Fricke pointed out that spread of varroa around the world has been mostly due to queen transfer. Purchasing queens from known infected areas is bound to result in infection. At present the varroa levels are low but spring will require treatments. Fortunately, Aluen Cap is now available. This treatment will not contaminate honey and will be easy to use. Beekeepers wishing to not use synthetic chemical treatments will have the oxalic acid treatment and along with brood breaks will be successful. Beekeepers must be careful, as I have seen on YouTube a clip recommending purchasing oxalic acid from Bunnings. This may contain heavy metals and nasty poisons. 100% clean oxalic acid is required.

A beekeeper that has reported finding two varroa at Inverleigh believes they came from drifting drones. The first one was in a wash and the next week he applied oxalic acid vapour and one was found on the sticky mat. His hives on the outskirts of Inverleigh did

not have drones or drone brood when shifted to the red gum [Eucalyptus camaldulensis] honey flow as they had suffered heat damage previously, but he noticed many drones appeared in his hives, possibly transferring the varroa.

A beekeeper when hearing of the varroa being found at Bellbrae shifted his bees to Torquay and inadvertently transferred varroa to a new area.

I am glad the Geelong Beekeeping Club is taking the varroa incursion seriously and the sub-committee is working well to educate members. I am still amazed by the number of beekeepers or new beekeepers that have very little knowledge of varroa. The first two to three years will need constant attention to your bees.

Washing bees for varroa detection is a timeconsuming operation for beekeepers with many hives. Marking queens is very important as I expect many unmarked queens will be killed in the washes (as I discovered). I have found a piece of heavy white plastic works well for me as I place it near the hive and have another look for the queen and after the flying bees have departed, give it a shake so the bees group in the centre and the poor young bees are tipped into a spray paint lid, the extra bees overflow into the brood nest over the open brood box frames the required number of bees tipped into the wash bottle. I prefer using detergent, as it is not a fire risk and does not damage the seals of the wash bottle. Alcohol damages the seals and they leak. I have been surprised at how much debris can be in a wash. I found my first varroa and was in disbelief at hives located south of Ballarat. Small specks of debris confused the wash results. Magnification is required to make sure of what you are seeing. I resorted to using a blue veil and my offsider's coffee mug to strain the liquid and take a photo with the mobile phone at 5 times magnification and still could not work out what the debris was. I could not find my 100mm round make-up mirror which would have been a help [previously John has noted this good idea that placing a magnifying make up mirror on a flat surface can enable you to inspect your varroa shaker from underneath without having to actually get underneath it yourself, as you can look in the mirror from your comfortable standing position -ed.].

President's Report

Lindsay Callaway · Page 5 · June 2026

Dear Victoria's beekeepers. It's your industry. It's your VAA. And VAA is your collective voice and peak body.

Beekeepers want the same things... • We want healthy bees. • We want viable businesses. • We want practical solutions. • We want to win the fight against varroa. • We want a future worth handing to the next generation.

However, for too long, parts of our industry have been distracted by division and fragmented thinking. Having and sharing different points of view is essential, and it is necessary that we each bring our views to the table. The future we build to adapt to the paradigm shift of varroa will succeed only if we build that future together.

After more than twelve months in this role, I have enormous respect for every VAA president who has come before me. Your Victorian Apiarists' Association is relatively small in structure, yet massive in importance to agriculture, food security and the future of apiculture in this state. And yet, despite the challenges, I have never been more optimistic.

Adversity has a way of revealing what matters. Varroa has now established itself across Victoria. Fuel costs, seasonal pressure, increasing operating costs and regulatory complexity continue to test commercial and recreational beekeepers alike. The emergence of resistant mite strains only sharpens that challenge further. But I believe this challenge will ultimately make us stronger.

Varroa is forcing us to evolve. It is forcing us to collaborate. It is forcing us toward smarter, safer and more sustainable management systems. And in many ways, this transition will strengthen Victoria's beekeeping for generations to come.

VAA exists to ensure that the transition ahead happens with Victoria's beekeepers at the centre of every decision. By putting beekeepers first, VAA provides a home for all Victoria's beekeepers. By putting beekeepers first, VAA provides a voice for all Victoria's beekeepers. This has been a year of delivery, progress and strategic momentum for Victoria's beekeeping.

Over the past twelve months, your VAA has delivered practical outcomes across education, advocacy, research, industry support and association growth. Thanks to a lot of work by a lot of people, here are some key results/highlights…

Strategic direction and renewal

• Established the framework for the VAA Strategic Plan.

• Formal articulation of VAA principles of One Home. One Voice. One Guardian.

• Clearer alignment of advocacy, education and member service priorities.

Biosecurity and education • Delivery of the Advanced Commercial Varroa Workshop at Swanpool. • Delivery of the Specialist Varroa Workshop at Cairnlea. • Continued growth of HiveMeet as Victoria's monthly digital learning platform. • Expansion of the VAA's online education and recorded resource library.

Industry capability and support

• Launch of the Apiary Business Navigator Program.

• Development of the Peer-to-Peer Beekeeper Support framework.

• Practical support for Victorian beekeepers navigating varroa transition.

Advocacy and policy

• Initiated and led the landmark DEECA Strategic Roadmap Workshop.

• Progress through the Ag-Chem Working Group toward practical treatment reform.

• Continued advocacy on public land access, prescribed burning and beekeeper service fees.

• Stronger representation of honey bee food security issues to government and agriculture.

Research and Long-term stewardship

• Progression of the Box Ironbark Forest research grant.

• Strengthening of the Resources subcommittee.

• Development of practical long-term floral resource stewardship pathways.

Community and association strength

• Record momentum leading into the 2026 Annual Conference.

• Strong sponsorship support and growing industry backing.

• Outstanding Melbourne Royal Show volunteer contribution.

• Continued branch engagement and regional leadership growth.

These are not isolated activities. They are strategic building blocks. Together, they represent a VAA with purpose and momentum.

One home. VAA continues to strengthen its role as the home for all Victoria's beekeepers.

Across branches, workshops, digital education and volunteer programs, we are seeing stronger engagement from recreational, sideliner and commercial beekeepers alike.

Importantly, we are seeing younger beekeepers stepping forward. This matters. Because the long-term strength of our association depends on renewal.

President's Report, cont'd

One Voice. VAA has worked hard over the past twelve months to ensure Victoria's beekeepers are heard clearly and credibly. Government is listening. Agriculture is listening. Policy makers are listening. That has only been possible because our advocacy is increasingly united, evidence-based and solutionsfocused.

One Guardian. Varroa remains the defining challenge of our generation. VAA will continue fighting to ensure Victoria's beekeepers have access to treatment pathways that are legal, practical, safe and effective. We must move quickly toward a future where every Victorian beekeeper, regardless of scale, has confidence in the treatment options available to them.

This work is well underway and will remain one of VAA's highest priorities.

A stake in the ground

The next twelve months must be a year of renewal. My hope is that we continue to see younger leaders step forward, ready to serve, support and shape the beekeeping industry via VAA. My expectation is that Victoria's beekeepers continue to unite around practical, evidence-based varroa management. My commitment is that VAA will continue pushing for the systems, education and regulatory settings required for our industry to thrive.

I remain incredibly proud to serve as VAA president. Thank you to every board member, volunteer, sponsor, branch representative and member who has contributed to this year's progress. Together, we will continue to build something stronger.

Kind regards, Lindsay Callaway President Victorian Apiarists' Association Inc.

Bags Of Dead Bees

By Kris Fricke

In the official training we provided we were told to tell beekeepers after an alcohol wash to securely bag the dead bees and dispose of them into the waste management system. The legal basis for this is presumably one or both of the following:

The Australian Honey Bee Industry Code of Practice §7.1 – A beekeeper must not allow a used hive, part of a used hive (including frames, combs, honey or beeswax) or an appliance containing honey to be exposed in a manner or under conditions likely to attract robber bees; including during transportation. 1

The Livestock Disease Control Act of 1994 2 also has some related provisions:

§ 52 Exposure of bees to infected articles etc.

A person must not expose—

any hive infected with disease; or

any beeswax, fittings or other articles which are infected with disease or have been taken from or used in conjunction with any hive infected with disease— in any manner that will allow access by bees to the hive or articles unless the hive or articles have been disinfected.

A person must not expose or extract any honey which is infected with disease in any manner which will allow access to the honey by bees.

A person must not expose any pollen collected from bees, which is infected with disease, in any manner that will allow bees to access the pollen. A beekeeper must take all reasonable precautions to ensure that any honey, honeycomb, fittings or other article on or in which honey is deposited and which is in the person's possession or under the person's control does not remain exposed in such a manner or under such conditions that would provide bees with access to the honey.

The Livestock and Disease Control Act of 2017 3 provides additional legal requirements for beekeepers but doesn't touch on this question.

These are very good reasonable regulations everyone should adhere to in order to prevent the spread of disease. They are often paraphrased as ―do not leave any hive material exposed,‖ which is generally close enough, but an astute reader may note that dead bees soaked in alcohol are neither likely to attract robber bees, nor are they infected with disease.

Consider that 1200-2400 bees are born in a hive every day during the season, and therefore 1200-2400 bees are also dying every day. Compared to this normal background delivery of dead bees into the environment by every single active hive, the addition of 300 dead bees is not significant. Yes, it's one ~30g clump, which is similar to a dead mouse, which, incidentally, is also a naturally occurring thing of little impact.

A thin film of alcohol will evaporate in less than an hour, and alcohol is a naturally occurring substance that definitely does not have any long-lasting toxic consequences for the environment. Dumping out the remaining alcohol could kill the grass it's dumped on, but if dumped into bare dirt should have no discernable negative consequences.

And so, if, after doing an alcohol wash you make a small depression in the dirt with your heel, dispose of the dead bees into it, and cover it again, there would be no environmental or legal hazard.

Obviously, there's nothing wrong with collecting all your dead bees in a bag to throw in your trash at home, but on the ecological balance that does introduce nonbiodegradable plastic into the waste system and the dead bees are also now taking up space in the waste system rather than quickly decomposing in the natural environment.

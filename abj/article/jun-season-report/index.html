Season Report

John Edmonds · Page 28 · June 2026

The start of May has seen some rain, which will encourage farmers to sow crops and give the forests a drink. It has become very dry around the Geelong region and the grass that grew from the past rains has started to become brown. Few flowering plants can be found, fortunately the hives have stored pollen. The cold weather has persisted and it has been too cold to do more testing for varroa. Most hives have been packed down for winter and some honey is still over clearer boards waiting to be removed. The shed is full of boxes waiting to be extracted, but we seem to be so busy with other chores like packing honey. Honey sales are variable with customers looking for value. Start of May

Winter flowering ironbark [Eucalyptus sideroxylon] has started to flower and combined with the warm spell of weather the hives filled an ideal box in a fortnight. The first week of June is forecast to be cooler but this winter is predicted to be warmer and drier than usual. So, the decision will be ―do we put the bees to sleep or keep them working?‖

A beekeeper told me that his hives were packed down to eight frames of bees for winter, but at a recent inspection he discovered they were now six frames of bees. Is it the poor autumn or varroa treatments causing the bees to drop strength? Another beekeeper is reluctant to reduce his double hives as the brood box has a lot of brood and little honey stores.

Hive scales report hives in the Geelong area vary from 48 to 65kg. Flowering Observations:

Bees handle yellow gum [Eucalyptus leucoxylon] flowering during the winter better than the ironbark. When the bees have extracted frames, they can fill them quickly with sloppy nectar during the cold, and that can cause fermentation problems. Presently the yellow gum [Eucalyptus leucoxylon] throughout Central Victoria is looking a picture and yielding well.

I am finding that washing bees for varroa testing takes a lot of time, but is necessary to evaluate varroa numbers.

I have taken an interest in soil fertility and the forest floor needs the bark and leaves dropped from the trees to form the compost to nourish the plants. I think the constant environmental burning of the forests has a negative effect and reduces the health of the forest. I have watched a messmate / stringybark forest [Eucalyptus obliqua] for many years that has had constant burning, and I observe the trees have not had a general budding for over a decade – just the odd tree forms budding and bud drop seems to occur.

The composition of these forests has changed since the 1980s when I took out the bee sites. Prickly wattle / kerosene bush [Acacia paradoxa] has become rampant due to its germination being aided by fire. Incidentally I have seen LandCare regenerative plantings that are planting this shrub which tends to become a weedy thicket. It flowers at the same time as the almonds, providing pollen.

A beekeeper who has become ill decided to not attend to his hives. He asked if I would inspect them. Despite being in a good location on the edge of Geelong the bees have not done well this season. The reasons are that the sugar gum [Eucalyptus cladocalyx] failed, the dry conditions caused weeds that normally flower not to, and, most importantly, the hives have not been requeened. I noted that the hives that swarmed and successfully requeened themselves were his best hives. Fortunately, no varroa. 13/5/2026

Inspected an apiary containing large established hives and five frame full depth nucleus hives. Due to the very dry April, the smaller colonies had minimal brood and fewer bees were in the colonies than last month, but the stronger colonies still had good brood and bee strength. The larger hives obviously were flying further than the small hives. Fresh nectar and pollen was present. All hives were given my protein patties and it was interesting to see the bees instantly start feeding on them, and the entrances of the nucleus hives had bees excited and congregating on the fronts of the hives. I would like to remind beekeepers that the Royal Geelong Show has a honey competition and for the first time the Sunbury show will have a honey competition.

I judged the J Beekeeping Club (Sunbury) honey and beeswax competition. The honey exhibits were very well presented with excellent clean honey. The flavours were obviously from mixed floral sources due to the difficult season. Some of the honey was starting to candy and not clear in appearance. Some had been poured just before the competition so had not settled. The wax candles were very difficult to judge as they were exceptionally good and all very similar in quality, although many different designs. Resulting in many equal first, seconds and thirds. The Geelong Beekeeping Club competition saw improved presentation of exhibits and quality. Flavours of the honey were mostly mixed floral sources but two samples were very good and mostly red gum [Eucalyptus camaldulensis].

Over the past few years, I have had good results from giving the bees protein patties just before winter. Faba bean flour has high protein content and Agrisea has amino acids and minerals and vitamins. I use these as a base and now experimenting with additional supplements. When varroa becomes a problem feeding bees becomes a recommended way to help the bees. I am presently doing a test of four different recipes and will monitor which the bees prefer to eat.

I place protein patties made from faba bean flour, soy flour, Agrisea - and some sugar to combine the mixture - in the hives before winter and find they create brood and healthier bees for spring. Bees nibble them and I try and place them on top of the brood frames. I did this already and returned a fortnight later and observed nearly all the protein patties had been consumed.

Despite keeping bee hives in a remote location and making sure no bee products were taken into that area for over three years I was devastated to find a varroa mite in one hive wash. Initially, you think ―it cannot be that small, surely it is something else.‖ But using the phone to magnify the small critter and seeing the front legs protruding the shell, you know your beekeeping is about to change. Looking at it under a microscope you can see the legs underneath and you have no doubts. Since then, I have improved the washing technique and using multiple wash containers (copied from Randy Oliver) I number the hives and the jars and bring them home for closer inspection by straining the liquid. The bumpy roads also aid the separation of the mites from the bees. I conclude

that varroa can appear anywhere now as I have found it in places where I thought there was very few bee hives located.

A very good night was held at Werribeeks May meeting. Bill Backendorf outlined the Personal Protection Equipment that beekeepers need to wear when using oxalic acid treatments, especially for vaporizing. Many beekeepers are taking risks and using oxalic acid that is not approved food grade. Timber cleaner grade contains nasty chemicals. Full face masks with eye and nose protection and the correct activated charcoal cartridge with a particulate cartridge clipped on top is recommended. Inspect inside the mask that the valve is in place and the mask is not deformed. Do a test to make sure you have a seal on your face, and be clean shaven.

The question was asked ―when do you know the cartridge needs replacing?‖ The cartridges have a two-year life span, but it is important to understand that this is the shelf life. The actual use time is measured in hours. You should keep track of the hours you have used a cartridge. If you have not kept track, estimate the number of times you used it and the minutes each time, and be generous in your estimate. It is better to replace a cartridge too early than too late. Store the cartridge sealed in a ziplock bag between uses.

Gloves must be strong and no holes or splits as handling acid treatments can result in burns that are not apparent till some hours later. No leather gloves. Nitrile types best.

Tyvek suits must be worn as invisible oxalic acid vapours condense on your clothing and if placed in the vehicle with you can become active, vaping you! It was said a beekeeper put the hot vaporizer in the car with his dog and the remaining fumes killed the dog.

Using the oxalic acid strips is much safer and does not require the mask but don't sniff the strips.

Using brood breaks will be essential.

It was suggested the threshold for varroa treatment be lowered to three varroa mites and treat immediately.

Another tip was that the seal on the varroa wash bottle leaks, but a shoe lace jammed into the lid ring effectively seals the wash bottle.

Reports from New South Wales and Queensland are horrific about the devastation varroa and small hive beetles are causing. I expect supply of bee hives for almond pollination will become a huge problem and supply of queen bees also. A beekeeper told me that his last shipment of queens from New South Wales were duds and all failed. I guess the varroa and treatments damaged the queens.

All the hive scales report minimal increase in weight and most going negative especially after rain events.

Yellow legged hornets are becoming a big problem in some countries overseas.

France, Spain and now Britain are unable to stop the spread. Auckland, New Zealand, discovered an incursion and are busy implementing high tech methods to try and eradicate the pest. We must be careful and not allow this pest to establish itself here.

An article from The Beekeepers Quarterly (BKQ) March 2026 explores the storage of pollen in the hive and the part ―the art of lactic fermentation‖ plays. Briefly, fungicides from crop sprays will destroy yeast cells which lead to the spoilage of the bee bread. Formic acid is often used as a miticide to treat varroa and the acid disrupts the pH conditions and affects some of the bacterial and yeast species. This presents another thing for us to consider when treating varroa.

Another very interesting article in the same issue of BKQ was about Tongan beekeeping. A summary of it: all synthetic and organic treatments of varroa are banned by the Tongan government. The tropical climate is very humid which suppresses the varroa. This results in a reduction of varroa reproduction. Consequently, the bees swarm all through the year. The Tongan bees have adapted by using swarming to reduce the effects of varroa. When a hive swarms 60 to 70% of the varroa leave the hive with the swarm. A broodless period occurs while the queen is mating. This also occurs with the swarm. It has been found that bees kept in small hives are more productive. The recommended hive size is a ¾ size (WSP) brood nest and same size honey super over a queen excluder. Some swarms will be weak and die. An experiment by Loftus et al (2016) 1 of 12 small hive and 12 large hives resulted in just one third of varroa numbers in the small hives. Of the large hives only two survived of the original 12. The smaller colonies are used mainly for pollination of crops, and do not make much honey.

Large colonies produce large amounts of honey.

I find this fascinating as we are told that without treatments the hives will die. Perhaps beekeepers need to move away from the yellow Italian bees that make large amounts of brood. More selection of Carniolan queens that make small brood nests, yet can be as productive as hives with large amounts of brood.

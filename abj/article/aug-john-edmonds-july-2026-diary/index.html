John Edmonds' July 2026 Diary

John Edmonds · Page 38 · August 2026

The start of July is cold winter weather with much needed rain. The area around Geelong has now received enough moisture to promote growth in canola and pasture plants and weeds. Even on the cold days we enjoy a few hours of sunshine in the afternoon. It is amazing to see how quickly the canola plantlets have developed from little two to four leaves to now small plants. It is pleasing to see the attitude of farmers now appreciating the benefit of honey bee pollination. A farmer calculated that the bees would increase his yield by 100 tonnes. A farmer said ―you can put your bees anywhere but not in the backyard as Mum would not like that.‖

8/7/2026 – Today I was amazed at the quick growth of the canola plants, good rainfall, warm soil and sunny afternoons spurring the growth. I expect an earlier than usual flowering. Swans are still grazing on the young canola plants but I see the farmer parks his utes in the middle of where the swans feast. They will not go near the vehicles. Eastern grey kangaroos love eating canola and each year I see greater numbers as they breed prolifically. I have kept bees on this farm since 1982 and it has only been over the past 10 years that three or four kangaroos have now become multiple mobs of up to 70 kangaroos of all sizes.

I enjoy looking for the brolga birds. About four years ago only a pair were present and had a pair of offspring. This spring I counted nine brolgas in the group. They are very tame and not scared of people taking photos and watching them. Being light grey they are hard to see as they wander through the paddocks finding grain, insects, mice, frogs and even eat roots of plants. Just on dusk I have seen them dancing to impress their partner.

As I travel around Geelong, I see lemon scented gums [Corymbia citriodora] budded for late spring flowering. Nearly every year these street trees flower and provide an excellent light honey. The spotted gum is not consistently budded this year, but some trees look good. The bees gather pollen but I have never noticed the very dark honey in the hives. This tree is sought after in New South Wales as it is a very good yielder and makes strong bees. My friend took his bees to the spotted gum at Batemans Bay and harvested honey but paid dearly for it with many stings. He was amazed at how many small hive beetles invaded his hives.

It seems the sugar gum [Eucalyptus cladocalyx] this season was a failure, and bees just survived.

Soursob [Oxalis pes-caprae] has started flowering and I find it hard to evaluate how much benefit the bees gain from it. It can yield a thin nectar and pollen and breed the bees. I think the weather is very important to whether the bees can harvest it. This weed has increased its spread greatly over the past few years. My neighbor blamed rabbits for spreading it.

It seems the trees are mixed up and some flowering early, bottlebrushes [Callistemon spp.], Manchurian pears [Pyrus ussuriensis], yellow box [Eucalyptus melliodora] and red box [Eucalyptus polyanthemos].

Thankfully most are still holding their budding.

Reports of canola crops that have started flowering in the northern areas of Victoria will give the bees a boost prior to almond pollination. Plenty of canola crops can be found from very small plantlets to cabbage bushes nearly ready to flower. If the canola flowers when the weather is cold, bees only breed. The canola requires warmth to yield a honey flow.

I doubt that the hives will have had a brood break this winter, a few quick checks have discovered at least two frames of brood. I expect hives to be very variable depending on the autumn floral conditions and quality of the queen. A Mildura beekeeper told me that he has hives with six frames of brood and doubles full of bees at the start of July. I expect varroa will have been breeding to higher numbers. Good luck stopping them swarming on the Almond pollination.

10/7/2026 – As I drove through the Brisbane Ranges, I saw the first wattle blooms opening. My grandmother's birthday was August 18th and she said her father on her birthday would bring in armfuls of golden wattle [Acacia pycnantha] to brighten the house. They lived beside the You Yangs. A story I find amazing was her sister and Nana would walk the three and a half miles to the Flinders Peak school walking along the split rail fences because wild cattle and horses resided in the You Yangs and possibly were dangerous. They would climb through the fence rails to safety. Her father sought shelter in a boxthorn [Lycium ferocissimum] bush for a day while a wild bull was after him.

I placed hives in a cold locality with flowering ironbark hoping the bees would go out of brood and the varroa if any would not breed. So far it appears the bees are full to the brim but confined to the bottom box leaving the honey over the super unoccupied. As soon as the canola flowers they will be shifted to breed up, and the honey extracted.

When I inspect the beehives, I mark any that are aggressive, so they can be requeened. I have noticed that often the next time they are well behaved, why the difference? An article in the June American Bee Journal reports that bees that are hungry are likely to have nasty attitudes. They refer to these bees as ―hangry.‖

I hear some beekeeping clubs are struggling to find speakers for their meetings. I suggest playing some of the YouTube beekeeping clips and reviewing the mistakes. For example, using varroa treatments without personal protection! I saw one where the beekeeper could not find the queen despite her clearly being in the center of the frame.

Around where I live, we are seeing the rapid expansion of Geelong with new houses being built. The developers knock down the existing trees and make a bare paddock to sub divide. Unfortunately, I observe that the new houses take up nearly all the allotment and the gardens have very little floral sources for bees to for-

Many areas of Geelong have had new plantings of Manchurian pears which flower early spring and seem to only provide pollen. I suspect they may increase swarming. When I travel through Werribee's new suburbs they have excellent honey yielding trees planted in the many reserves and nature strips. Yellow box, grey box [Eucalyptus microcarpa], red box, red gum [Eucalyptus camaldulensis], yellow gum [Eucalyptus leucoxylon], spotted gum [Corymbia maculata], and ironbark [Eucalyptus tricarpa]. This should be done in all Victorian new housing suburbs.

A very interesting article in the June 2026 American Bee Journal by Randy Oliver the American scientist beekeeper. He had been testing the pH levels of oxalic acid treatments on the surface of bees after oxalic acid treatments. He found anomalies in the experiment results. He concluded that it was caused by bee venom being acidic. It was neutralizing the oxalic acid. Further reviewing research papers revealed that worker bees smear bee venom over themselves during selfgrooming. Bee venom is antimicrobial and contains peptides and melittin. It was concluded that venom bathing may be used by bees to reduce varroa infestation. It was found that bees that did not have varroa had less venom on themselves. Queens and drones did not have a coating of venom. The question is do Apis cerana also use venom to protect themselves? There are variations in varroa mite fertility, could this be due

to bee venom? I have found bee venom ointment to be useful in reducing pain from arthritis.

My friend pollinates GMO seed crops near Hay NSW. He says the GMO canola does not yield a honey crop. Another friend pollinated the GMO canola seed and said he was never going back as the bees came off weaker and lighter in weight. He was very lucky as while walking along looking at the flowering canola a monster-size king brown snake struck at his thigh, fortunately he stepped sideways and the snake missed as they cannot move sideways once they start the strike. Beware of standing behind a snake as they can spring backwards the length of their body.

In the Brisbane Ranges a large colony of cockatoos reside. Presently they are chewing the flowers off the ironbark trees and also snipping off the ends of branches. Under some trees a large amount of debris has formed as the birds prune the trees. Under the flowering trees the ground can be white with flowers.

The flowering yellow gums around my property are full of hundreds of rainbow lorikeets [Trichoglossus moluccanus]. They noisily screech to each other and fly quickly to fresh trees. Their numbers seem to have increased dramatically as 14 years ago my late wife would feed them and the most that came to the feeder was 36.

This report is finishing early July as I will be away visiting Queensland.

A sting of hope for Parkinson's disease Scientists investigate bee venom as a potential companion to conventional Parkinson's therapy CHINESE MEDICAL JOURNALS PUBLISHING HOUSE CO., LTD.

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder caused by the loss of dopamine-producing neurons, leading to motor symptoms such as tremors, rigidity, slowed movement, and impaired balance, as well as cognitive decline. Although current treatments help relieve symptoms, they cannot stop disease progression. Levodopa (L-DOPA), given with carbidopa, remains the standard treatment for PD by restoring dopamine levels and improving motor function. However, its benefits often decline with long-term use, and complications such as motor fluctuations and dyskinesias may develop. These limitations have prompted the search for adjunct therapies that could improve treatment outcomes. In this context, researchers from the University of Guadalajara investigated whether bee venom could improve the therapeutic effects of L-DOPA/carbidopa in a mouse model of PD. Bee venom contains biologically active compounds, including melittin, phospholipase A2, and apamin, which have demonstrated anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroactive properties. ―We aimed to explore whether bee venom could potentiate the effects of standard therapy in a 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA) model of Parkinson's disease,‖ explained lead author Professor Alma Karen Lomeli-Lepe from the University of Guadalajara. The study was published in Neuroprotection on May 20, 2026. The study was published in Neuroprotection on May 20, 2026… Overall, the results suggest that bee venom may amplify the functional benefits of L-DOPA/carbidopa, improving both motor and cognitive outcomes in experimental Parkinson's disease. ―Our findings demonstrate promising behavioral benefits of bee venom as an adjunct to L-DOPA/carbidopa therapy, but further research is needed to understand the underlying mechanisms,‖ noted Prof. Lomeli-Lepe. While additional research is required, these findings highlight a promising avenue for developing adjunct therapies aimed at improving outcomes for people living with Parkinson's disease.

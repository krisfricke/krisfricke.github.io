Facebook Roundup, May 2026

By Kris Fricke

This month, particularly in the Commercial Beekeeping Australia group, there are numerous long and serious posts about how something just has to change in government support because the current situation now with chemical resistant mites is unsustainable. Compared to other months it definitely feels like people are reaching a breaking point. Several posts recount lifelong stories as multi generational beekeepers before transitioning to dire reports of their recent losses and finally calls for either legalization of more cost effective treatment options or subsidies for the struggling industry. Rather than summarize these posts, which wouldn't do them justice, I will reproduce in full several good posts the authors have given me permission to do so with.

But first, Min Fuller of Bee Services, Grafton NSW, who has been actively advocating on behalf of beekeepers, has posted a poll she would like beekeepers to answer to help her advocacy efforts. (This is not the same survey as the one AHBIC has also recently put out).

Min Fuller, in Commercial Beekeeping Australia, May 21, 22, 25th:

The beekeeping industry is often seen as high risk. But the reality is this: inaction guarantees failure.

What have I got to lose?

Or perhaps the real question is:

What will Australia lose?

According to the Australian Bureau of Statistics, there were only 1,263 registered commercial beekeeping businesses in Australia as of June 2025.

To put that into perspective, commercial beekeepers account for only around 4% of registered beekeepers in Australia.

Yet that 4% is responsible for an estimated $14.2 billion in pollination services and approximately 80% of Australia's honey production, according to the Australian Honey Bee Industry Council.

Commercial beekeepers are the backbone of Australia's food security.

We pollinate the crops that feed this country - blueberries, raspberries, blackberries, macadamias, passionfruit, almonds, avocados and countless other industries that depend on healthy, managed honey bees.

But what happens if commercial beekeepers collapse?

What happens when financial pressure, rising input costs, biosecurity threats, bee losses, labour shortages, and the mental toll of carrying enormous responsibility become too much?

Because without commercial beekeepers, there are no pollination services. Without pollination, there is reduced production, higher food prices, lower yields and increasing pressure on Australia's agricultural system.

Now, to answer the question: what do I have to lose?

I was born into beekeeping. I grew up in this industry. I have a deep passion for supporting and speaking up for commercial beekeepers because this is not just a job to me - it is my life.

My parents entered the beekeeping industry before I was born. My father has worked in beekeeping for 47 years, surviving droughts, floods, fires, market pressures, and biosecurity incursions that have challenged our industry time and time again.

Today, our family business employs 10 full-time staff, including my father, who is now semi-retired. Alongside my partner, brother-in-law, and wider family support network, we manage around 5,000 hives, producing honey and pollinating 38 farms across the North Coast, including blueberries, raspberries, blackberries, passionfruit and macadamias.

This is more than a business.

It is livelihoods. It is regional jobs. It is food production. It is food security.

And if commercial beekeepers continue to fall under financial pressure, Australia will lose far more than honey.

Australia risks losing the people who quietly help feed the nation. 1

I do not want to create fear or panic, but I do want commercial beekeepers to be prepared.

From what we have personally experienced in our own operation, I believe synthetic-resistant varroa mites may already be far more widespread across parts of NSW and Queensland than many realise. What I have seen in our own hives has honestly shaken me.

Varroa alone is devastating - but what concerns me most is how quickly colonies can collapse once hives start struggling when treatments are not working. In my personal opinion, based on what we have observed in the field, resistant mites may also be associated with increased viral pressure or secondary health issues within hives. This is my opinion and observation only, and I acknowledge this still needs to be proven through further industry and laboratory research.

What I can say with confidence is this: hive losses can happen fast.

Faster than many of us are prepared for.

I genuinely believe this could shake the commercial beekeeping industry harder than the original varroa incursion if you are not ready for it.

For commercial beekeepers who are yet to experience varroa firsthand - please don't assume time is on your side. Prepare now. Monitor aggressively. Review treatment plans, rotate management strategies, track hive health closely, and have honest conversations about contingency planning.

We cannot afford to be complacent.

A shortage of hives for almond pollination and other critical pollination events is, in my view, a very real possibility if colony losses continue at the scale some of us are already seeing.

This is not fear-mongering. This is a beekeeper speaking openly about what we are seeing, because I don't want others caught unprepared.

Facebook Roundup, May 2026, cont'd

Please prepare your business, your bees, and your people.

The commercial beekeeping industry is under pressure - and we need to support one another through what may be ahead. 2

One of my biggest fears for the Australian beekeeping industry is not just losing bees - it's losing the people.

Some of the older commercial beekeepers in our industry carry decades of knowledge, experience and practical skills that cannot be learned from a textbook, a Facebook group or a quick online search.

They have lived through droughts, floods, fires, changing seasons, biosecurity threats, market pressures and the constant challenge of keeping bees alive.

They know country, flowering patterns, bee behaviour, honey flows, pollination logistics and how to survive the hard years.

That knowledge matters.

If commercial beekeeping continues to become financially unsustainable, if stress, losses and pressure continue to push people out, we risk losing generations of experience overnight. And once that knowledge disappears, we may never get it back.

At the same time, we desperately need younger people entering this industry.

We need young commercial beekeepers willing to learn, adapt and carry this knowledge forward. But how do we expect the next generation to step in when many are watching businesses struggle, hives die, costs rise and families fight just to survive?

Commercial beekeeping is not just about honey. It is about pollination, food security, agriculture and protecting an industry that quietly supports so much of Australia.

If we don't support commercial beekeepers now - old and young - we risk losing not only an industry, but the experience, resilience and future generations needed to keep it alive. 3

Anita Long, founder of Australian Youth Beekeeping, May 28th

The future of beekeeping is in our hands, let's not drop the ball!

Min has opened up a great discussion on legacy, succession and ultimately the concern for our beekeeping industry future. Discussions that need to happen now and at all levels of our industry.

We need to be careful not to frame the future of beekeeping only through loss.

Yes, we are at a critical point. Australia is facing challenges that have already played out in other parts of the world. Declining beekeeper numbers, financial pressure, pests, diseases, biosecurity threats, low honey

Facebook Roundup, May 2026, cont'd

prices and the loss of experienced people are not issues unique to us. In many ways, this situation was predictable because other countries have already shown us what happens when an industry waits too long to invest in the next generation.

But that also means we can learn from them.

Through my Churchill Fellowship, looking at youth development and pathways into beekeeping, one thing became very clear. Many strong youth beekeeping programs around the world were not created because everything was going well. They were created because industries reached crisis point and realised they could not afford to lose their people, their skills or their knowledge.

In some countries, that meant school programs. In others, it meant junior clubs, mentoring pathways, accreditation programs, practical training, or structured ways for young people to connect with experienced beekeepers. The strongest examples were not just about teaching children to like bees. They were about building long-term pathways into the industry.

That is where Australia needs to be far more intentional.

The intergenerational transfer of knowledge is critical. Experienced beekeepers hold practical knowledge that cannot be replaced, we know that. But that knowledge can only be passed on if we create places where younger people can safely access it, ask questions, observe, learn, handle equipment, understand seasons, and be included in real beekeeping conversations.

That does not always require a major national program, although that would and could be world leading. It can start at every level, through clubs, associations, schools, commercial operators, mentors, training providers and industry bodies making young and emerging beekeepers genuinely welcome.

And I do not believe this is all doom and gloom.

Young people entering beekeeping now are not learning in the industry as it was 30 years ago. They are learning in the industry as it is today. They are learning with varroa, biosecurity pressures, changing seasons, financial strain, pollination demands and global uncertainty. This will be their normal. They will adapt, innovate, learn from overseas, build networks, use new tools, and develop skills shaped by the world they are actually inheriting.

Every generation of beekeepers looks back and remembers the ―good old days‖ before certain pests, diseases or pressures arrived. But the next generation will not simply be trying to recreate the past. They will be learning how to keep bees in the conditions we have now, while carrying forward the knowledge of those who came before them.

That is why investing in young people is not a feelgood extra. It is industry protection.

If we make access to knowledge, education, mentoring, accreditation and practical experience easier, we give young people a reason to stay. We also give experienced beekeepers the opportunity to pass on what they know before it is lost.

Australia does not need to start from scratch. Other countries have faced these challenges before us and have built programs in response. We should be learning from them, adapting what works, and investing now, before the loss becomes irreversible.

I know many of you have followed youth work being done in beekeeping and support it wholeheartedly. It's certainly more than seeing kids in bee suits or cute photos of kids beekeeping. There are a good handful of people right here in Australia changing the landscape for young beekeepers and I proudly put my hand up as one of those, but there could be so many more.

I am sure many commercial beekeepers get sick of seeing our posts or stories of the opportunities we are creating for young people, we certainly don't do that for likes or clicks or even fundraising (at the moment) it is in the hope some of you with all the knowledge will one day find a way to give it to a younger beekeeper as your legacy. 4

Corinne Jordan of Bee Lady Apiaries, in Beekeeping Victoria, May 18 th

Lessons from a Queensland Beekeeper:

To Melbourne and Victorian beekeepers - please do not underestimate what is coming.

Queensland is now learning the hard way what an active Varroa invasion wave looks like, and my concern is that many southern beekeepers still think this will behave like a manageable ―normal mite problem.‖ It may not.

Yes, Victoria has a cooler climate and stronger winter brood breaks than subtropical Queensland. That may slow mite growth for part of the year.

But Melbourne also has:

• High hive density,

• Explosive spring brood expansion,

• High colony interaction through drifting and robbing,

• Large numbers of colonies living within close flight range of each other.

That combination can allow mite numbers to escalate extremely quickly once reinvasion begins.

If I could restart in Queensland knowing what I know now, I would:

Monitor drone brood constantly — not just alcohol washes

Remove and inspect drone brood regularly during spring build-up

Assume brood infestation is higher than alcohol washes suggest

Start treatments earlier, not later

Alcohol wash again about a week after synthetic treatments go in to confirm they are actually working

Avoid overreliance on synthetics before resistance establishes

Use Formic Pro sparingly and carefully - it can knock colonies around hard

Preserve survivor genetics before the invasion wave peaks

One of the biggest mistakes I believe Australia is making is assuming overseas management models directly translate to our conditions.

What we appear to be dealing with in Queensland is:

• resistant mites,

• continuous reinvasion,

• rapid brood infestation,

By the time you start seeing widespread collapse across an area, you are already behind.

I genuinely hope Victoria's cooler winters buy you time.

Use it wisely.

Australian Bee Journal

John Edmonds’ July 2026 Diary

By John Edmonds

The start of July is cold winter weather with much

needed rain. The area around Geelong has now re-

ceived enough moisture to promote growth in canola

and pasture plants and weeds. Even on the cold days

we enjoy a few hours of sunshine in the afternoon. It is

amazing to see how quickly the canola plantlets have

developed from little two to four leaves to now small

plants. It is pleasing to see the attitude of farmers now

appreciating the benefit of honey bee pollination. A

farmer calculated that the bees would increase his yield

by 100 tonnes. A farmer said ―you can put your bees

anywhere but not in the backyard as Mum would not

like that.‖

8/7/2026 – Today I was amazed at the quick growth of

the canola plants, good rainfall, warm soil and sunny

afternoons spurring the growth. I expect an earlier than

usual flowering. Swans are still grazing on the young

canola plants but I see the farmer parks his utes in the

middle of where the swans feast. They will not go near

the vehicles. Eastern grey kangaroos love eating canola

and each year I see greater numbers as they breed pro-

lifically. I have kept bees on this farm since 1982 and it

has only been over the past 10 years that three or four

kangaroos have now become multiple mobs of up to 70

kangaroos of all sizes.

I enjoy looking for the brolga birds. About four

years ago only a pair were present and had a pair of

offspring. This spring I counted nine brolgas in the

group. They are very tame and not scared of people

taking photos and watching them. Being light grey they

are hard to see as they wander through the paddocks

finding grain, insects, mice, frogs and even eat roots of

plants. Just on dusk I have seen them dancing to im-

press their partner.

As I travel around Geelong, I see lemon scented

gums [Corymbia citriodora] budded for late spring

flowering. Nearly every year these street trees flower

and provide an excellent light honey. The spotted gum

is not consistently budded this year, but some trees look

good. The bees gather pollen but I have never noticed

the very dark honey in the hives. This tree is sought

after in New South Wales as it is a very good yielder

and makes strong bees. My friend took his bees to the

spotted gum at Batemans Bay and harvested honey but

paid dearly for it with many stings. He was amazed at

how many small hive beetles invaded his hives.

It seems the sugar gum [Eucalyptus cladocalyx] this

season was a failure, and bees just survived.

Soursob [Oxalis pes-caprae] has started flowering

and I find it hard to evaluate how much benefit the bees

gain from it. It can yield a thin nectar and pollen and

breed the bees. I think the weather is very important to

whether the bees can harvest it. This weed has in-

creased its spread greatly over the past few years. My

neighbor blamed rabbits for spreading it.

It seems the trees are mixed up and some flowering

early, bottlebrushes [Callistemon spp.], Manchurian

pears [Pyrus ussuriensis], yellow box [Eucalyptus mel-

liodora] and red box [Eucalyptus polyanthemos].

Thankfully most are still holding their budding.

Reports of canola crops that have started flowering

in the northern areas of Victoria will give the bees a

boost prior to almond pollination. Plenty of canola

crops can be found from very small plantlets to cabbage

bushes nearly ready to flower. If the canola flowers

when the weather is cold, bees only breed. The canola

requires warmth to yield a honey flow.

I doubt that the hives will have had a brood break

this winter, a few quick checks have discovered at least

two frames of brood. I expect hives to be very variable

depending on the autumn floral conditions and quality

of the queen. A Mildura beekeeper told me that he has

hives with six frames of brood and doubles full of bees

at the start of July. I expect varroa will have been

breeding to higher numbers. Good luck stopping them

swarming on the Almond pollination.

10/7/2026 – As I drove through the Brisbane Ranges, I

saw the first wattle blooms opening. My grandmother‘s

birthday was August 18th and she said her father on her

birthday would bring in armfuls of golden wattle

[Acacia pycnantha] to brighten the house. They lived

beside the You Yangs. A story I find amazing was her

sister and Nana would walk the three and a half miles to

the Flinders Peak school walking along the split rail

fences because wild cattle and horses resided in the

You Yangs and possibly were dangerous. They would

climb through the fence rails to safety. Her father

sought shelter in a boxthorn [Lycium ferocissimum]

bush for a day while a wild bull was after him.

I placed hives in a cold locality with flowering iron-

bark hoping the bees would go out of brood and the

varroa if any would not breed. So far it appears the

bees are full to the brim but confined to the bottom box

leaving the honey over the super unoccupied. As soon

as the canola flowers they will be shifted to breed up,

and the honey extracted.

When I inspect the beehives, I mark any that are

aggressive, so they can be requeened. I have noticed

that often the next time they are well behaved, why the

difference? An article in the June American Bee Jour-

nal reports that bees that are hungry are likely to have

nasty attitudes. They refer to these bees as ―hangry.‖

I hear some beekeeping clubs are struggling to find

speakers for their meetings. I suggest playing some of

the YouTube beekeeping clips and reviewing the mis-

takes. For example, using varroa treatments without

personal protection! I saw one where the beekeeper

could not find the queen despite her clearly being in the

center of the frame.

AHBIC Chair & CEO Reports

Over the last couple of months, AHBIC has been

attending the state conferences, culminating in Tasma-

nia followed by the AHBIC AGM. All state confer-

ences have been well attended this year, demonstrating

the continued thirst for knowledge as we navigate the

challenges of managing varroa. Most conferences have

had a strong mix of domestic and international speakers

and extensive trade displays.

As we move through winter and beekeepers begin to

open hives in preparation for spring and pollination

events, we are receiving mixed reports on hive strength

and numbers. Varroa and the impacts of the resistant

varroa population continue to significantly influence

hive numbers, with many beekeepers reporting large

colony losses. Unfortunately, current jurisdictional data

systems are not timely enough to capture these colony

losses in real-time, making the Colony Loss Survey

even more important this year. The survey is due to

open in September/October, and it is more important

than ever to participate because this data influences

government decisions.

2025/2026 Season

The 2025/2026 season has been one of the toughest

periods Australian beekeepers have witnessed. Histori-

cally we referenced weather patterns, prospects and

production, today the focus is on varroa and business

viability.

The discovery of a new incursion of varroa that is

resistant to the two main chemical classes has come as a

shock for industry. Our thoughts are with those opera-

tors who learnt they were dealing with the resistant

strain too late. This was not operator error, rather being

caught off guard by something that we did not imagine

occurring for decades. Now that the beekeeping com-

munity understands and recognises that we are dealing

with a different beast, we as beekeepers can adapt and

adopt alternative treatment methods moving forward.

With any crisis comes opportunity. For the willing,

the future is bright. Several reports recently published

explain that Australia will be short of bees for pollina-

tion events in the foreseeable future. Less bees will

result in less honey being produced, which will result in

Australian honey becoming a premium. Australian bee-

keepers are renowned for their resilience and adaptabil-

ity. Those who can combine the craft of successful

beekeeping in Australia with varroa management will

prosper.

Honey Bee Industry Emergency Roundtable

The current varroa situation facing our industry does

not fit within any pre-existing government framework

that allows governments to easily activate industry sup-

port. We are no longer in the biosecurity emergency

response framework, as we have transitioned to man-

agement, and varroa is not considered a natural disaster.

As a result, we are seeing governments struggle to find

ways to support industry. To complicate the situation,

beekeepers are mobilising, sending letters to ministers,

speaking in the media and making representations to

local MPs, all of which is raising the profile and im-

portance of the challenge. However, inconsistencies in

messaging and in what we are asking of government

have slowed their response.

The AHBIC team are very much in touch with the

beekeepers on the ground. Across the AHBIC staff and

executive, there is a constant stream of communication

that results in AHBIC having a true understanding of

what is happening. Behind the scenes, the AHBIC

team have constant discussions on the impacts that bee-

keepers are experiencing using this information to act.

AHBIC is listening. The challenge is determining how

to help industry and what to advocate for that is realis-

tic. This is a common challenge for any agricultural

representative body. To be taken seriously, AHBIC

must go to government with a clear, well thought out,

achievable request.

To provide greater clarity to governments, AHBIC,

in collaboration with the Queensland Beekeepers Asso-

ciation and the Queensland Government, has held a

Honey Bee Industry Emergency Roundtable. The aim

of the roundtable is not to hold a talkfest, but to run an

action-oriented process that first agrees on a set of pri-

oritised, deliverable actions and then present those ac-

tions to governments seeking agreement.

The first of the two meetings, the technical work-

shop held on 30 June, brought together 40 representa-

tives from industry, governments, RDCs and pollina-

tion-dependent sectors, with half of the participants be-

ing beekeepers. A total of 55 actions gathered from

industry across 10 themes were considered by the work-

shop. Participants worked through each action, ensur-

ing it was deliverable, impactful and feasible under

government policies and regulations. They then priori-

tised the themes and actions.

These 55 actions were then distilled into 11 actions

based on the technical workshop prioritisation and feed-

back. The 11 actions have been presented to all levels

of government, seeking agreement at a Decision Mak-

ers Forum on 28 July. This forum includes representa-

tives from each jurisdiction at the most senior levels,

ensuring decisions can be made.

While unconventional, this approach provides a

clear set of agreed actions, strengthens the case for sup-

port and will identify any barriers to agreement. The

post-forum, industry-facing paper will provide beekeep-

ers with a clear lobbying document they can use to en-

sure consistency in messaging.

But that also means we can learn from them.

Through my Churchill Fellowship, looking at youth

development and pathways into beekeeping, one thing

became very clear. Many strong youth beekeeping pro-

grams around the world were not created because eve-

rything was going well. They were created because

industries reached crisis point and realised they could

not afford to lose their people, their skills or their

knowledge.

In some countries, that meant school programs. In

others, it meant junior clubs, mentoring pathways, ac-

creditation programs, practical training, or structured

ways for young people to connect with experienced

beekeepers. The strongest examples were not just about

teaching children to like bees. They were about build-

ing long-term pathways into the industry.

That is where Australia needs to be far more inten-

tional.

The intergenerational transfer of knowledge is criti-

cal. Experienced beekeepers hold practical knowledge

that cannot be replaced, we know that. But that

knowledge can only be passed on if we create places

where younger people can safely access it, ask ques-

tions, observe, learn, handle equipment, understand sea-

sons, and be included in real beekeeping conversations.

That does not always require a major national pro-

gram, although that would and could be world leading.

It can start at every level, through clubs, associations,

schools, commercial operators, mentors, training pro-

viders and industry bodies making young and emerging

beekeepers genuinely welcome.

And I do not believe this is all doom and gloom.

Young people entering beekeeping now are not

learning in the industry as it was 30 years ago. They are

learning in the industry as it is today. They are learning

with varroa, biosecurity pressures, changing seasons,

financial strain, pollination demands and global uncer-

tainty. This will be their normal. They will adapt, in-

novate, learn from overseas, build networks, use new

tools, and develop skills shaped by the world they are

actually inheriting.

Every generation of beekeepers looks back and re-

members the ―good old days‖ before certain pests, dis-

eases or pressures arrived. But the next generation will

not simply be trying to recreate the past. They will be

learning how to keep bees in the conditions we have

now, while carrying forward the knowledge of those

who came before them.

That is why investing in young people is not a feel-

good extra. It is industry protection.

If we make access to knowledge, education, mentor-

ing, accreditation and practical experience easier, we

give young people a reason to stay. We also give expe-

rienced beekeepers the opportunity to pass on what they

know before it is lost.

Australia does not need to start from scratch. Other

countries have faced these challenges before us and

have built programs in response. We should be learn-

ing from them, adapting what works, and investing

now, before the loss becomes irreversible.

I know many of you have followed youth work be-

ing done in beekeeping and support it wholeheartedly.

It‘s certainly more than seeing kids in bee suits or cute

photos of kids beekeeping. There are a good handful of

people right here in Australia changing the landscape

for young beekeepers and I proudly put my hand up as

one of those, but there could be so many more.

I am sure many commercial beekeepers get sick of

seeing our posts or stories of the opportunities we are

creating for young people, we certainly don‘t do that for

likes or clicks or even fundraising (at the moment) it is

in the hope some of you with all the knowledge will one

day find a way to give it to a younger beekeeper as your

legacy.4

Lessons from a Queensland Beekeeper:

To Melbourne and Victorian beekeepers - please do

not underestimate what is coming.

Queensland is now learning the hard way what an

active Varroa invasion wave looks like, and my con-

cern is that many southern beekeepers still think this

will behave like a manageable ―normal mite problem.‖

It may not.

Yes, Victoria has a cooler climate and stronger win-

ter brood breaks than subtropical Queensland. That

may slow mite growth for part of the year.

But Melbourne also has:

• High hive density,

• Explosive spring brood expansion,

• High colony interaction through drifting and rob-

bing,

• Large numbers of colonies living within close

flight range of each other.

That combination can allow mite numbers to esca-

late extremely quickly once reinvasion begins.

If I could restart in Queensland knowing what I

know now, I would:

Monitor drone brood constantly — not just al-

cohol washes

Remove and inspect drone brood regularly dur-

ing spring build-up

Assume brood infestation is higher than alcohol

washes suggest

Start treatments earlier, not later

Alcohol wash again about a week after synthet-

ic treatments go in to confirm they are actually working

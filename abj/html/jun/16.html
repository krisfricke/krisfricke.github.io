Facebook Roundup, May 2026

By Kris Fricke

This month, particularly in the Commercial Bee-

keeping Australia group, there are numerous long and

serious posts about how something just has to change in

government support because the current situation now

with chemical resistant mites is unsustainable. Com-

pared to other months it definitely feels like people are

reaching a breaking point. Several posts recount life-

long stories as multi generational beekeepers before

transitioning to dire reports of their recent losses and

finally calls for either legalization of more cost effective

treatment options or subsidies for the struggling indus-

try. Rather than summarize these posts, which wouldn‘t

do them justice, I will reproduce in full several good

posts the authors have given me permission to do so

with.

But first, Min Fuller of Bee Ser-

vices, Grafton NSW, who has been ac-

tively advocating on behalf of beekeep-

ers, has posted a poll she would like bee-

keepers to answer to help her advocacy 

efforts. (This is not the same survey as

the one AHBIC has also recently put

out).

Min Fuller, in Commercial Beekeeping Australia,

May 21, 22, 25th:

The beekeeping industry is often seen as high risk.

But the reality is this: inaction guarantees failure.

What have I got to lose?

Or perhaps the real question is:

What will Australia lose?

According to the Australian Bureau of Statistics,

there were only 1,263 registered commercial beekeep-

ing businesses in Australia as of June 2025.

To put that into perspective, commercial beekeepers

account for only around 4% of registered beekeepers in

Australia.

Yet that 4% is responsible for an estimated $14.2

billion in pollination services and approximately 80% of

Australia‘s honey production, according to the Australi-

an Honey Bee Industry Council.

Commercial beekeepers are the backbone of Austral-

ia‘s food security.

We pollinate the crops that feed this country - blue-

berries, raspberries, blackberries, macadamias, passion-

fruit, almonds, avocados and countless other industries

that depend on healthy, managed honey bees.

But what happens if commercial beekeepers col-

lapse?

What happens when financial pressure, rising input

costs, biosecurity threats, bee losses, labour shortages,

and the mental toll of carrying enormous responsibility

become too much?

Because without commercial beekeepers, there are

no pollination services. Without pollination, there is

reduced production, higher food prices, lower yields

and increasing pressure on Australia‘s agricultural sys-

tem.

Now, to answer the question: what do I have to lose?

I was born into beekeeping. I grew up in this indus-

try. I have a deep passion for supporting and speaking

up for commercial beekeepers because this is not just a

job to me - it is my life.

My parents entered the beekeeping industry before I

was born. My father has worked in beekeeping for 47

years, surviving droughts, floods, fires, market pres-

sures, and biosecurity incursions that have challenged

our industry time and time again.

Today, our family business employs 10 full-time

staff, including my father, who is now semi-retired.

Alongside my partner, brother-in-law, and wider family

support network, we manage around 5,000 hives, pro-

ducing honey and pollinating 38 farms across the North

Coast, including blueberries, raspberries, blackberries,

passionfruit and macadamias.

This is more than a business.

It is livelihoods. It is regional jobs. It is food pro-

duction. It is food security.

And if commercial beekeepers continue to fall under

financial pressure, Australia will lose far more than

honey.

Australia risks losing the people who quietly help

feed the nation.1

I do not want to create fear or panic, but I do want

commercial beekeepers to be prepared.

From what we have personally experienced in our

own operation, I believe synthetic-resistant varroa mites

may already be far more widespread across parts of

NSW and Queensland than many realise. What I have

seen in our own hives has honestly shaken me.

Varroa alone is devastating - but what concerns me

most is how quickly colonies can collapse once hives

start struggling when treatments are not working. In my

personal opinion, based on what we have observed in

the field, resistant mites may also be associated with

increased viral pressure or secondary health issues with-

in hives. This is my opinion and observation only, and I

acknowledge this still needs to be proven through fur-

ther industry and laboratory research.

What I can say with confidence is this: hive losses

can happen fast.

Faster than many of us are prepared for.

I genuinely believe this could shake the commercial

beekeeping industry harder than the original varroa in-

cursion if you are not ready for it.

For commercial beekeepers who are yet to experi-

ence varroa firsthand - please don‘t assume time is on

your side. Prepare now. Monitor aggressively. Review

treatment plans, rotate management strategies, track

hive health closely, and have honest conversations

about contingency planning.

We cannot afford to be complacent.

A shortage of hives for almond pollination and other

critical pollination events is, in my view, a very real

possibility if colony losses continue at the scale some of

us are already seeing.

This is not fear-mongering. This is a beekeeper

speaking openly about what we are seeing, because I

don‘t want others caught unprepared.

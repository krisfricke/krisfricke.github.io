Honey competitions.

I would like to remind beekeepers that the Royal

Geelong Show has a honey competition and for the first

time the Sunbury show will have a honey competition.

I judged the J Beekeeping Club (Sunbury) honey and

beeswax competition. The honey exhibits were very well

presented with excellent clean honey. The flavours were

obviously from mixed floral sources due to the difficult

season. Some of the honey was starting to candy and not

clear in appearance. Some had been poured just before

the competition so had not settled. The wax candles were

very difficult to judge as they were exceptionally good

and all very similar in quality, although many different

designs. Resulting in many equal first, seconds and

thirds. The Geelong Beekeeping Club competition saw

improved presentation of exhibits and quality. Flavours

of the honey were mostly mixed floral sources but two

samples were very good and mostly red gum [Eucalyptus

camaldulensis].

Over the past few years, I have had good results from

giving the bees protein patties just before winter. Faba

bean flour has high protein content and Agrisea has ami-

no acids and minerals and vitamins. I use these as a base

and now experimenting with additional supplements.

When varroa becomes a problem feeding bees becomes a

recommended way to help the bees. I am presently doing

a test of four different recipes and will monitor which the

bees prefer to eat.

I place protein patties made from faba bean flour, soy

flour, Agrisea - and some sugar to combine the mixture -

in the hives before winter and find they create brood and

healthier bees for spring. Bees nibble them and I try and

place them on top of the brood frames. I did this already

and returned a fortnight later and observed nearly all the

protein patties had been consumed.

Despite keeping bee hives in a remote location and

making sure no bee products were taken into that area for

over three years I was devastated to find a varroa mite in

one hive wash. Initially, you think ―it cannot be that

small, surely it is something else.‖ But using the phone

to magnify the small critter and seeing the front legs pro-

truding the shell, you know your beekeeping is about to

change. Looking at it under a microscope you can see

the legs underneath and you have no doubts. Since then,

I have improved the washing technique and using multi-

ple wash containers (copied from Randy Oliver) I num-

ber the hives and the jars and bring them home for closer

inspection by straining the liquid. The bumpy roads also

aid the separation of the mites from the bees. I conclude

that varroa can appear anywhere now as I have found it

in places where I thought there was very few bee hives

located.

A very good night was held at Werribeeks May

meeting. Bill Backendorf outlined the Personal Protec-

tion Equipment that beekeepers need to wear when using

oxalic acid treatments, especially for vaporizing. Many

beekeepers are taking risks and using oxalic acid that is

not approved food grade. Timber cleaner grade contains

nasty chemicals. Full face masks with eye and nose pro-

tection and the correct activated charcoal cartridge with a

particulate cartridge clipped on top is recommended.

Inspect inside the mask that the valve is in place and the

mask is not deformed. Do a test to make sure you have a

seal on your face, and be clean shaven.

The question was asked ―when do you know the car-

tridge needs replacing?‖ The cartridges have a two-year

life span, but it is important to understand that this is the

shelf life. The actual use time is measured in hours. You

should keep track of the hours you have used a cartridge.

If you have not kept track, estimate the number of times

you used it and the minutes each time, and be generous in

your estimate. It is better to replace a cartridge too early

than too late. Store the cartridge sealed in a ziplock bag

between uses.

Gloves must be strong and no holes or splits as han-

dling acid treatments can result in burns that are not ap-

parent till some hours later. No leather gloves. Nitrile

types best.

Tyvek suits must be worn as invisible oxalic acid

vapours condense on your clothing and if placed in the

vehicle with you can become active, vaping you! It was

said a beekeeper put the hot vaporizer in the car with his

dog and the remaining fumes killed the dog.

Using the oxalic acid strips is much safer and does

not require the mask but don‘t sniff the strips.

Using brood breaks will be essential.

It was suggested the threshold for varroa treatment

be lowered to three varroa mites and treat immediately.

Another tip was that the seal on the varroa wash bot-

tle leaks, but a shoe lace jammed into the lid ring effec-

tively seals the wash bottle.

Reports from New South Wales and Queensland are

horrific about the devastation varroa and small hive bee-

tles are causing. I expect supply of bee hives for almond

pollination will become a huge problem and supply of

queen bees also. A beekeeper told me that his last ship-

ment of queens from New South Wales were duds and all

failed. I guess the varroa and treatments damaged the

queens.

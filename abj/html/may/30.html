FACEBOOK ROUNDUP by Kris Fricke

Effects of Heat on Honey (April 5th)

A question in Beekeeping Victoria about the affects of heat on honey garnered a discussion with 27 comments.

The poster asked specifically for scientifically backed studies rather than “Fred says” or “google says.” Someone

pointed to a paper1 about enzymes degrading at 40-60C, and concerns about HMF (hydroxymethylfurfural), a

common indicator of quality loss in honey, which meaningfully accumulates at 37-40°C (ie a hot shed) over weeks

to months, but at hotter temperatures, reaches the EU defined maximum limits in 80 hours at 60°C or 6 hours at

100°C.

I‟m only just seeing this discussion and only sat down to write brief summaries here but I would add that the

“volatile aromatics,” the subtle and delicate flavors that make a high quality honey amazing,2 frequently evaporate just over 40°

C, and the same browning affect that happens when you caramelize sugar or brown onions (the “Maillard reaction”) begins to

happen slowly at over 35°C (taking months to reach problematic levels),3 measurable after days at 50°C and rapidly over 60°C.4

Amitraz resistance (April 14th)

Not surprisingly the announcement of miticide resistant bees got beekeepers talking, with both ex-

tensive discussions on the Central Victorian Apiarist Association page and Commercial beekeepers.

In the latter Robert Seagrave offered more information on the origin of the hives he says were the

first to show this resistance. In both of these discussions, as you can imagine, a great deal of frustra-

tion was expressed.

Why Can’t We Be Like Canada? (April 22nd)

The posting of an article from Bee Culture magazine about the approval of oxalic glycerin strips in Canada incited a discussion

regarding why the APVMA hasn‟t done similar for us here. A particular comment by Nicholas Charters got numerous likes and

“well said” responses so I‟ll reproduce it here in full:

Hi Rod, the beekeepers in the industry that do what they want with disregard for others [are what] we should all be concerned

about. We acknowledge what has been done to date by AHBIC and the govt employees who have the industry at heart.

Of course we need an approval authority to regulate chemical use in the Ag industry. However this discussion is not asking for

own use of DDT or even synthetic chemicals! In Canada their equivalent of AHBIC obtained approval for own use OA method-

ology from their equivalent to APVMA.

The question raised therefore asks can we in Australia seek to do the same. We have an industry that has been under sustained

stress. The discussion comes from a posture of seeing good beekeepers lose hundreds of hives, profitability, businesses, relation-

ships etc

Given the evolving situation with mite resistance to Bayvarol and Apivar, and looking ahead at the approved treatment schedule

it is pertinent to question does it adequately accommodate:

• Sequential knock down options during spring and summer

• Cost effective treatments

• Year round honey boxes in subtropical climates (autumn bees currently wanting to be in triples not singles)

• Rotating 4 approved treatments per year where resistance is present

• We all want an industry that can:

• Reduce synthetic reliance with associated residue risks

• Use organic treatments wherever possible

• Sustain profitability and still produce premium table honey rather than transition to a pollination-only business model

• Maintain treatment compliance records

• legitimately sign off on annual Code compliance

Those of us who have seen our colleagues suffer and fail whilst trying to work with current options have a responsibility to

forewarn that there needs to be consideration of further changes for the industry to survive and prosper and we need our

representative bodies to listen and back us.

AHBIC CEO Danny LeFeuvre‟s article “Miticide Registrations, APVMA and the Role of AHBIC“ which also appears in this

same issue (page 31) is effectively the other side of this same discussion.

Unanswered Questions About Oxalic Acid (April 24th)

A very spirited discussion about how Oxalic Acid actually works developed in response to the posting of a snip-

pit of a Randy Oliver article on “Aussie Apiarists Online.” 23 people participated in this discussion putting for-

ward numerous theories, though ultimately it is not conclusively known.

The honey bee hive functions like a superorganism,

with each bee acting as a cell in a larger body. Com-

munication within the hive is almost certainly at the

heart of collective colony intelligence. Bees communi-

cate with a range of behaviours ranging from simple

sharing of pheromones and food, to performing com-

plex dances to pass on learning to worker sisters.

By communicating to other bees what they have

learned, bees are extending the memory of their experi-

ence to other members of the colony and this is in ef-

fect a „colony memory‟ that extends beyond the life of

individual bees.

Communication is also an important factor in how a

hive makes collective decisions. Some of these deci-

sions occur only once a year, others occur many times

each day. A swarm‟s choice of a new home is the clas-

sic case of collective decision making. Individual scout

bees find potential sites and communicate the location

by dancing. Other bees follow the directions and return

to dance themselves. If a worker had a negative experi-

ence at the new site, such as close encounter with a

predator, they will stop other workers from dancing

directions to that location. When enough bees are

dancing the same location, that is a majority vote in

favour, and the decision is made.

Collective action is based on many individual deci-

sions. Every field bee makes a daily or even hourly

decisions on whether to forage for nectar, pollen or wa-

ter. These decisions are mostly based on things we

cannot see or perceive. It is likely that things like tem-

perature, humidity and the balances of specific hor-

mones and pheromones combine, but the collective

result of the individual decisions is the complex behav-

iour that we see in the hive. For example if humidity in

the brood nest started to fall, field workers about to

leave on a foraging flight might decide to collect water.

While the humidity remains low, more and more forag-

ers would divert to water collection until the humidity

level increased.

It is important to note that the intelligence that we

measure in bees is directly relevant to their biological

needs. This is easier to explain with something that

bees don‟t do as well as their relatives, the social wasps

– that is logical problem solving. In laboratory experi-

ments, honey bees are able to learn that food from a

blue container is better than food from a red container

and that the red container is better than a yellow con-

tainer. But they are unable to translate that learning to

know that the blue container is better than the yellow.

This sort of logic does not have a role in the hive.

Some social wasps can solve these simple logic prob-

lems, which is probably related to their different social

behaviour. These wasps have multiple queens in one

colony and the queens develop a dominance hierarchy.

An individual wasp most likely uses the logic ability in

working out their position in the hierarchy.

Most of the scientific study of intelligence has fo-

cussed on worker bees, but it makes sense that drones

and queens must also use their intelligence to enhance

their specific roles in the hive. Drones are capable of

sophisticated learning but it is finely tuned to their spe-

cific biological mission – finding and mating with a

queen.

There is recent evidence suggesting that the learning

and memory capabilities of queens are highly advanced

and potentially play a crucial role in long-term colony

stability. Queens may be even more intelligent than

their worker daughters. This may play some role in

longer term colony memory.

With all the evidence of the intelligence of bees, it

would be easy to over-play the evidence and draw con-

clusions that are unlikely to be true. A bee‟s brain has

perhaps a million neurons. Imagine that the bee‟s brain

is a 300-page book with a very good index. The bee can

easily find and use information from its brain, which is

a marvel of miniaturised processing power. The bee

applies a relatively small set of instinctive rules to the

information coming from their senses, and their

memory, resulting in the complex behaviour that we

observe.

But for all the marvels of bee behaviour, they are

still working with an insect brain with a very limited

capacity and their intelligent behaviour is very closely

tied to their life and survival.

A human brain has 86 billion neurons, equivalent to

the millions of books in the US Library of Congress.

While our brain takes more time to find and process

this massive information base, we have much larger

capacity for memory and learning, including learning

about things that have nothing to do with our survival.

It is possible in laboratory experiments to train

worker bees to recognise and distinguish between hu-

man faces. The bees are almost certainly using the

parts of their brain that recognised flowers, and this

pattern recognition works with the patterns of human

faces. But it a huge leap to suggest that bees learn to

recognise their owners.

I don‟t believe that bees have any awareness that

humans exist. We are just a part of the landscape–

something that is there and something that sometimes

represents a threat. There is a mythology surrounding

bees and beekeeping that I don‟t subscribe to. To my

mind, this is largely a construct of our human brain and

our propensity to look for a meaning behind everything

that we observe, even when there is no meaning there.

Beekeeping implications

In April 2024, some of the world‟s leading scientists

signed the New York Declaration on Animal Con-

sciousness, which concludes that there is a realistic

possibility of consciousness in insects, including honey

bees. It argues that where this possibility exists, we

have an ethical obligation to consider their welfare.

I believe that managing beehives can be ethical and

humane if beekeepers prioritise bee welfare. Opinions

clearly differ and some of our beekeeping decisions

involve complex ethical issues.

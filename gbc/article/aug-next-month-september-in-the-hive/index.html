Next month (September) in the hive

Gary Montgomery · Pages 14–15 · August 2026

September

What is it? Its Impact on the hive

Swarming is a bee colony's way of surviving and multiplying. A swarm occurs when the current (old) queen leaves the hive with approximately half of the worker bees. This loss of bees (5000+) seriously impacts on a hives capacity to gather nectar for the remainder of the season.

It may also upset neighbours who are subject to the swarming process, or who end up with the swarm establishing themselves as a new colony in their residence.

Swarming generally occurs in spring (Sept) through until early summer (mid December) although there are no guarantees of when swarming may commence!!

Initial stimulus for swarming occurs when there is little room in the hive for the queen to lay, the build up of brood is rapid, replacement queen cells are present in the hive and it is a warm, sunny day.

Most swarms occur between 10am / 2pm.

After a swarm emerges from a hive it will generally settle on a bush, post or tree branch anywhere from 5 to 150 metres from the hive. It may remain there for up to 3 days, whilst scout bees search out a suitable spot for the swarm to relocate to and commence a new colony.

Whilst most hives produce a “prime swarm”, some hives may also produce secondary (“after”) swarms if not quickly checked to remove any further queen cells which further depletes a hives productive capacity

After the prime swarm leaves the hive, a virgin queen will emerge and immediately kill any remaining queens not yet emerged from their cells. She takes several mating flights and then commences egg laying. This may be up to 3 weeks after the swarm has left, causing significant delay in the brood rearing process and nectar gathering capacity of the hive. Causes of Swarming

Where bees have access to significant amounts of good pollen, coupled with a light nectar flow, as frequently available in urban residential areas, or in rural areas from cape weed or a nearby canola crop, they are far more likely to swarm.

Lack of available room in the brood box will significantly increase the probability of swarming. The addition of a “super” to a single brood box will reduce, but not entirely eliminate the likelihood of swarming. A very careful check needs to be maintained to ensure adequate room is always available for the queen to lay eggs and the workers to store nectar. Warning signs of potential swarming

One of the key signs is completed queen cells. Shaped like a “peanut,” these are generally found along the sides, or bottom of comb in the brood box.

Queen cells constructed on the surface of comb, using a “worker cell” as the base, are usually a response to an emergency replacement situation of a queen in the hive.

These signs may also be accompanied by an inadequate amount of space in the brood box for the queen to continue laying, along with high worker bee and brood (nurse) bee numbers. Prevention of swarming

Swarming is a natural impulse of the beehive. You may be able to reduce the swarming impulse, but never entirely eliminate it!! Several factors can assist in minimizing the swarming impulse.

i) Reduce the number of drone cells in the hive. All hives naturally produce drone cells but excessive numbers are an inducement to swarming. It is prudent to minimize the number of drones in the hive.

This can be achieved by removing frames with large numbers of drone cells from the brood box to the super (assuming there is a queen excluder in place). Destroying drone cells has minimal impact as they will simply be rebuilt and reused.

ii) Destroy any swarm queen cells. Examination of any combs in the hive that the queen has access to approx. every 10/12 days is vital if you are to detect and remove any developing queen cells to minimize the opportunity for swarming.

iii) Maintain a young vigorous queen. As a young queen exudes more pheromones than an old, ageing queen, she exerts more control over the bees in the hive and inhibits their swarming tendencies. If a new, young queen is introduced into the hive, ensure she has adequate laying room.

iv) Congestion in the hive. This is generally the most common cause of swarming. Ensure that there is always plenty of room in the brood box for the queen to lay. If necessary, remove frames of brood from the brood box into the super and those frames in the brood box with empty drawn worker comb or foundation.

Alternatively, take some frames of brood from a crowded brood box, and place into another hive needing additional brood. If doing this, ensure that the brood being relocated are free from disease (AFB, EFB, etc) and mites!! Replace these frames as already described.

Excess combs of honey may be similarly removed, and replaced with fresh foundation or drawn comb, to create additional space.

v) Artificial Swarming.

This can be used to "trick" the hive into not swarming. There are a variety of methods available (See Aust. Beekeeping Guide pg 44) to achieve this.

Assuming you have a double hive, one of the simplest methods is as follows:

a) Remove five frames of brood, with bees, from the brood box and place into a spare super.

b) Replace these removed frames with fresh foundation, or drawn comb,

c) Make sure the queen is in the bottom box.

d) Place an excluder on the top of this box.

e) Place a super containing combs of honey, empty combs and some foundation on top of the excluder.

f) Put a division board with its entrance at the rear or side on top of what is now a twostorey hive.

g) Place the spare super used in a) that contains the frames of brood and bees, above this division board and introduce a capped queen cell or caged laying queen immediately.

GENERAL BEEKEEPING

Rearing Queens

By John Edmonds

A new queen each year will result in strong healthy bee hives, and if of docile manner it makes working the

bee hive a much quicker and more pleasureable pastime.

Most important is to have a quality breeder queen. Undesirable traits can be magnified if an ordinary queen

is used. In nature bees tend to revert to aggressive traits as they are more successful in protecting the

colony.

Mature drones of the correct age must be present before making the queen cells. When drones are hatching

then queen cells can be started. Drones require 14 days and have started flying to drone congregation areas.

Good floral conditions and supplementary feeding will result in premium queens. It is best when the bees are

gathering a steady flow of mixed sources pollens and a steady flow of nectar. It is important that the bees

can source pollen from plants of European sources, although I now think that supplementary feeding Agrisea

also provides the required ammino acids. Heavy honey flows will often see the bees refuse to make queen

cells. Sometimes bees are not inclined to be in the mood for making queen cells and will chew down the

started queen cells if the honey flow stops, weather is unsuitable and possibly cold nights.

Making a queenless hive will cause the bees to start drawing queen cells. I recommend after 2 or 3 days you

knock down the queen cells they have started from their own brood and collect the royal jelly with an artist

paint brush.

Wash the plastic queen cells to remove any oil film from the molding process. Best to place the queen cells

into the hive for a day prior to grafting for the bees to prepare the queen cells and warm them. Using the

artists brush smear a layer of royal jelly into the base of the queen cell cups.

Grafting the grubs require selecting the 12-to-24-hour grubs. (a small "c") It is important not to use the big

grubs as although the bees will make queen cells the queens will hatch earlier and kill her sisters and be of

poor quality. When the bees draw out queen cells from older grubs, the older the grub, the more worker bee

features the queen will have and the less ovaries.

Using a Chinese grafting tool lift the grub out of the frame cell and transfer to the plastic queen cell on top of

the royal jelly. Black plastic frames are much easier to graft from, and newly drawn frames are the hardest. If

the grubs are being fed profusely and resting on top of plenty of bee milk then it is much easier to graft the

grubs. Feeding sugar syrup, the night prior will enhance the production of bee milk.

I prefer to graft late afternoon and take care that the strong sunlight does not burn or dry out the grubs. I

graft the cells at the apiary at the back of the Ute so it is important to have quiet bees as it is easier if you do

not have to wear a veil. If the day is hot, I return the frame of selected brood back to the breeder hive after a

short time and select another frame so the brood is fresh and not over heated. Usually, the sunlight is ok for

good vision but during cloudy days or if you are working into the evening the wearing of a forehead light

makes it easier to see the grubs.

I prefer to draw approximately 16 queen cells per 8 frame full depth box of bees. I often see were

beekeepers draw many more queen cells but they will be inferior size small queens.

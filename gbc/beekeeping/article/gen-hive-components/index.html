Hive components

Pages 14–15 · General Beekeeping

Typical LANGSTROTH hive

The hive body or boxes holds 8 to 10 frames that are

standardized in length. The frames hold the foundation

and the honeycomb that is built on it.

Hive equipment manufacturers will often produce bodies

and supers that vary slightly. The differences between

manufacturers are generally 2mm or less.

A 'Queen Excluder' is a screen or mesh of a specific size

that allows normal bees to pass through, but because the

Queen is slightly larger, she can not pass through.

Placing a Queen Excluder between a brood box and a

honey box means that while the brood box may contain

both brood and honey, the honey box above the Queen excluder will only contain honey as the Queen can

not get through to lay eggs.

A 'Brood Box' and a 'Honey Box' from a construction point of view are the same physical box. The difference

is just in the positioning and function within the hive.

The 'Brood Box(s)' at the bottom of the hive is where the Queen lays her eggs and the young bees (brood) are

raised.

'Honey Box(s)' (sometimes called 'Honey supers') at the top of the hive are where honey is stored. Honey is

also stored in the lower Brood box, and if a Queen Excluder isn't used, some brood may be placed in the

Honey boxes.

FLOW Hive

Flow hives are essentially a 'honey extraction system' and

are very similar to Langstroth hives as far as base and

brood box is concerned. The big difference is in the honey

boxes/super's and frames which are a unique design that

allows the honey to be extracted by turning a tap and

without the need of removing the frame from the hive.

To extract honey from traditional (Langstroth) hives, the

frames are removed from the hive and the wax covering

the honeycomb is cut/scraped off and then the honey is

spun or dripped out.

To extract the honey from a Flow hive, a lever is inserted into the honey frame to temporarily shift the

position of the plastic comb components allowing the honey to flow out a tube inserted at the bottom.

The advantage of this system is that the frames remain in the hive and there is minimum disruption to the

bees. There are however a few disadvantages to this approach as well. It can be difficult to see if the comb is

capped and the majority of the honey ready to extract. If the honey is thick or crystallised it will not flow out,

and the frames will need to be removed from the hive and heated to enable the honey to flow.

Flow hives are considerably more expensive than traditional Langstroth hives.

GENERAL BEEKEEPING Rearing Queens

A new queen each year will result in strong healthy bee hives, and if of docile manner it makes working the

bee hive a much quicker and more pleasureable pastime.

Most important is to have a quality breeder queen. Undesirable traits can be magnified if an ordinary queen

is used. In nature bees tend to revert to aggressive traits as they are more successful in protecting the

colony.

Mature drones of the correct age must be present before making the queen cells. When drones are hatching

then queen cells can be started. Drones require 14 days and have started flying to drone congregation areas.

Good floral conditions and supplementary feeding will result in premium queens. It is best when the bees are

gathering a steady flow of mixed sources pollens and a steady flow of nectar. It is important that the bees

can source pollen from plants of European sources, although I now think that supplementary feeding Agrisea

also provides the required ammino acids. Heavy honey flows will often see the bees refuse to make queen

cells. Sometimes bees are not inclined to be in the mood for making queen cells and will chew down the

started queen cells if the honey flow stops, weather is unsuitable and possibly cold nights.

Making a queenless hive will cause the bees to start drawing queen cells. I recommend after 2 or 3 days you

knock down the queen cells they have started from their own brood and collect the royal jelly with an artist

paint brush.

Wash the plastic queen cells to remove any oil film from the molding process. Best to place the queen cells

into the hive for a day prior to grafting for the bees to prepare the queen cells and warm them. Using the

artists brush smear a layer of royal jelly into the base of the queen cell cups.

Grafting the grubs require selecting the 12-to-24-hour grubs. (a small "c") It is important not to use the big

grubs as although the bees will make queen cells the queens will hatch earlier and kill her sisters and be of

poor quality. When the bees draw out queen cells from older grubs, the older the grub, the more worker bee

features the queen will have and the less ovaries.

Using a Chinese grafting tool lift the grub out of the frame cell and transfer to the plastic queen cell on top of

the royal jelly. Black plastic frames are much easier to graft from, and newly drawn frames are the hardest. If

the grubs are being fed profusely and resting on top of plenty of bee milk then it is much easier to graft the

grubs. Feeding sugar syrup, the night prior will enhance the production of bee milk.

I prefer to graft late afternoon and take care that the strong sunlight does not burn or dry out the grubs. I

graft the cells at the apiary at the back of the Ute so it is important to have quiet bees as it is easier if you do

not have to wear a veil. If the day is hot, I return the frame of selected brood back to the breeder hive after a

short time and select another frame so the brood is fresh and not over heated. Usually, the sunlight is ok for

good vision but during cloudy days or if you are working into the evening the wearing of a forehead light

makes it easier to see the grubs.

I prefer to draw approximately 16 queen cells per 8 frame full depth box of bees. I often see were

beekeepers draw many more queen cells but they will be inferior size small queens.

I like to feed the bees approx 1 to 1.5 litres each day of 50/50 sugar syrup with Agrisea for the first few days,

this will result in more queen cells and better-quality queens. A leading Beekeeping researcher said "do not

buy queens from a queen breeder that does not feed the bees".

It is most important to keep good records of when you have made the graft as in 10 days from grafting the

queen cells will be ready to place into the nucleus hives you have made up. I find in strong warm hives the

cells will hatch approximately midday on the 11th day. In smaller less populous hives, the queen cells may

take an extra day to hatch.

When removing the queen cells from the bee hive it is important to be gentle and do not shake the bees off

the frame or jolt the queen cells during transport. The queen's wings are the last thing to develop and are

sensitive to rough handling.

I have been told do not place the queen cells on their side, must be kept upright but I have found them to be

still OK. Queen cells that are about to hatch are remarkably tough and can stand cooling slightly and this will

slow down the hatching time.

It is important to position and colour the nucleus bee hives so the queens can recognize and remember the

position and not stray into a foreign bee hive. Returning virgin queens have a bad habit of drifting into your

breeder hives and killing your breeder.

Nucleus bee hives are generally best if they are 1 frame of honey and stored pollen, 2 frames with brood, and

an empty frame making a 4 frame nuc.

Press the queen cell into the beeswax of the frame just above the brood and the bees will keep it warm. It

can be a good idea to use a conical cage to protect the queen cell or even the use of electrician's tape around

the side of the queen cells will prevent worker nasty bees from rejecting the new queen. The emerging

queen communicates with the worker bees and sometimes must say the wrong thing and is killed. Rarely the

queen cannot hatch as she is upside down in the queen cell.

The queen will emerge and takes about 7 to 10 days to mature for her mating flights. She will wait for days

above 18/20 degrees and little wind. She will fly further than the drones from her colony to drone congrega-

tion areas that remain in use every year. How and why these drone congregation areas exist is a mystery.

20,000 drones will circle around these drone congregation areas waiting for the virgin queen to fly through

the drone congregation area and the fastest and fittest drones will mate in the air approximately 60 meters

above ground. It only takes 2 seconds for the mating to take place and the drone dies instantly falling

backwards dead. The next drone removes the remnants of the previous drone and research shows approxim-

ately 15 drones will mate over one to two days with the queen. Usually, the queen only absorbs 10% of the

semen. This will last her for her lifetime. Drones fly a short distance, whereas the queen flies a longer

distance to mate. Drones can only stay away from the hive for 25 minutes and have to return for more fuel

(honey) and fly 4 to 6 times per day.

After 21 days it is a good time to inspect the nucleus and see if the queen has succeeded. Approximately

70% of the queens will be successful.

I prefer to wait 28 days plus until the brood starts hatching so I can see the results of the queens mating.

Research has shown that queens caught from the 28-day nucs have a better success rate in being accepted

in the new hive.

Another method for making queen cells is to have a double decker hive with only 4 frames of honey, pollen

and sealed brood, the rest of the box has 4 follower boards (timber cut into the shape of frames that reduce

the space in the box). The bottom board has a mesh base to allow the bees air. The entrance is closed. The

young bees from a strong double hive are shaken into the bottom box the older flying bees will return home

to the queen. The bees will cover the 4 frames and the rest hang as a cluster like a swarm under the frames

into the empty chamber below. The graft of queen cells is introduced and the bees kept locked in for 2 days

in a cool place (often inside a shed).

The bees panic and start making queen cells. The queen cells can then be transferred to a strong bee hive

that has a super over a queen excluder with honey and pollen frames and the center has unsealed brood to

attract the nurse bees through the queen excluder. The bees will finish the queen cells. It is thought that this

method makes the best queens.

Cloake method uses a strong double decker hive that has a queen excluder and cloake board which has a

slide and upstairs entrance. The slide can be inserted making the upper super queen less, the bees will start

drawing the queen cells and then after 2 days the slide is removed to allow the hive become a complete unit

and finish making the queen cells.

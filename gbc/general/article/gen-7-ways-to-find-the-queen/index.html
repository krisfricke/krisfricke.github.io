7 ways to find the queen

Pages 29–31 · General Beekeeping

(And one way to get a result without finding the queen)

1. Go the centre

Use minimal smoke, 1–2 puffs; take off the honey super and queen excluder.

Space frames apart in the middle of the brood box.

Scrape off burr comb on the side of the top bar.

Carefully lift one frame out of the middle, taking care not to squash bees.

Briefly scan over each side of this frame (like reading, taking about 1 minute). Place this frame in a spare

box and continue with the next frame.

The queen can be on the wall of the hive body or on the bottom board. The centre of the brood nest, on a

frame with eggs, is the most likely place to find her. Speed is an advantage because the old queen can hide

well. Have an empty cage handy: if you find her on the first frame you might recycle her into a drone layer or

an eggless hive later, if she has a good brood pattern or other good qualities. Once you've found her, shake

bees off combs, check for disease, manipulate combs, scratch honey in corners to make egg-laying space, lift

honey out to the top and replace with empty brood combs, introduce the cage and mark the front of the box

with code – date – breeder (W123).

2. Go the outside

Otherwise known as the light barrier or Linton Briggs method.

Place an empty box next to the hive.

Use minimal smoke, 1–2 puffs; take off the honey super and queen excluder.

With the sun over your left shoulder, remove the outside frame furthest from you, checking for the

queen before placing it in the empty box.

Remove the outside frame closest to you, again checking for the queen before placing it in the spare box.

While it is not usual to find the queen on the outside frames (it can happen), removing these frames first

creates a light barrier between the next frame and the hive wall. This confines the queen to the remaining

frames.

Before checking both sides of the next frame closest to you, glance down the face of the frame.

According to Linton the queen often stands taller than the rest of the bees, and can be spotted up to

20% of the time on the face of the frame before it is removed. Repeat for the remaining frames until the

queen is found.

3. Divide and conquer

Put an empty box next to the hive.

Take out half the frames and place them in the empty box. Put empty combs in each box.

You will then have 4 frames of bees and brood, and 4 frames of pollen, honey and empty combs, in each

box.

The next day one of the hives will have fanning bees at the front entrance (looking to attract the queen). The

queen will be in the hive with no fanning bees at the entrance. She will now be easier to find because you

know which box she is in and have only half the bees to search.

If you have 2 brood boxes, splitting them gives a similar result: half the bees with the queen in one box. The

beauty of this method is that it only takes seconds to do.

4. Last resort

This one is a last resort and perfect for a recycled queen; it is suitable to use on a drone layer or a stinging

hive.

Move the whole hive 10–20 metres or more, preferably behind landmarks. This will cause the bees to

drift back to their original position.

Shake all bees off the combs onto the ground and place all frames into a new hive body elevated on a

table. This ensures that the queen cannot re-enter the hive.

Replace this brood box on the original spot to collect all the field bees.

The queen will not be able to fly back.

Check 7 days later for queen cells, knock them off and introduce a caged queen or cell.

5. Drift method

Suitable for pallets or apiaries with pairs or rows of hives.

Move the hive to a new position a few metres away behind a landmark and turn the entrance 180 de-

grees.

This will cause all the field bees to return to the hive next door. Usually this is OK under good conditions.

The next day, or even a few hours later, you only have nurse bees and the queen on the brood frames;

this gives you the edge in finding the old queen.

6. Divide and divide again

Place an empty hive body next to the hive.

Use method 1 first.

Place four frames in each box with bees adhering. Pair up the frames. This should make the bees and the

queen go between either of the two pairs of frames, where it is darker. After 10 minutes look for the

queen, and remember to look on the wall and floor as well.

7. Strainer method

This one will demoralise the hive for a while, so should only be used if all else fails.

Move the hive onto the next hive and put an empty body on the original place. On top of that place a

super with the queen excluder screwed on the bottom (the strainer box).

Shake all the bees into this strainer box, then return the brood frames from the original box, under the

strainer, into the brood box on its original location.

With a little smoke, force the bees through the strainer back into the brood box.

You will only have drones and the queen left in the strainer. Use only cool smoke, so the bees don't panic

and run up the walls of the strainer.

A slight variant is the Bob McDonald method. Move the hive and put down a new bottom board, put a queen

excluder on the bottom, then place an empty box; shake all the bees in front of the original position, placing

the brood frames into this box. The bees will enter, moving up through the queen excluder to look after the

brood. When the hive has settled down, lift the brood box off the excluder and the queen will be trapped

between the excluder and the bottom board. Place this bottom board and excluder in a hessian bag and re-

move.

8. Supersedure method (autumn only)

This method involves splitting the hive to introduce queen cells, possibly leaving the old queen.

Shake all the bees off the brood combs into the old brood box and place the combs into a new super (not

to be done under a heavy honey flow, because you risk drowning bees in their nectar).

Fill the brood box with empty brood combs, replace the queen excluder, then place the box of brood on

top. Nurse bees will move up through the queen excluder to feed the young. This can be done up to 7

days before.

The old queen will occupy the empty combs and start laying there. After 6 hours or the next day the hive

can be split with a division board or taken away to a new site. Either way you introduce the new queen

into this brood box. Since the old queen has lost brood and bees she should be easier to find in the next

few days.

By introducing a queen cell placed in a cell protector (or hair roller) it should survive and hatch the next day.

When the virgin queen hatches she does not yet have queen pheromones, and the old queen is used to bees

walking over her and grooming her, so the chance of the old queen being killed by the virgin queen's sting is

very high. This method is widely used by commercial beekeepers overseas as it is very quick and should give

80% success, so I would split stronger hives to give over 100% laying young queens for the coming season.

For this to work we need a good drone population and a breeding flow of nectar and pollen.

National Bee Unit

Shook swarm

August 2024

Shook swarms involve transferring adult bees onto fresh foundation in

a clean hive and then destroying the old comb and any associated

brood. Shook swarms are both an effective means of controlling pests

and diseases and can be used to replace old combs. This fact sheet

describes how to perform a shook swarm.

Why shook swarms are used

In the UK, shook swarms are used to treat colonies suffering from low levels of the brood

disease, European foulbrood (EFB), replacing the use of antibiotics which are less

effective1. However, in the UK, EFB is a statutory notifiable disease and if EFB is

suspected in a colony, beekeepers are legally obliged to inform the NBU. Shook swarms

for the treatment of EFB are performed only by, or alongside, an Appointed Bee Inspector

(ABI). The protocol described below is for shook swarms where EFB is not present.

It has been shown that a shook swarm is effective to reduce levels of Varroa2, and can be

used to treat colonies suffering from heavy chalkbrood infection3. It is also well worth

performing a shook swarm if the colony has a lot of old and dirty brood frames.

When shook swarms can be carried out

The best time to carry out a shook swarm is between mid-March and mid-July, depending

on the locality. The colony needs a substantial population of young bees at the right age to

make wax; the majority of wax building bees are between 10 and 20 days old4. It is usually

too cool for bees to effectively draw new comb before March and the loss of brood can be

detrimental so early in the year. If a shook swarm is being used as a routine management

tool, then it is best to avoid the period when eggs are laid that will develop into bees that

will forage on the main honey flow. This will depend on the beekeeper’s geographical

region and experience of local main crops.

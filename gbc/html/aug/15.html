Queen cells constructed on the surface of comb, using a “worker cell” as the base, are usually a

response to an emergency replacement situation of a queen in the hive.

These signs may also be accompanied by an inadequate amount of space in the brood box for the

queen to continue laying, along with high worker bee and brood (nurse) bee numbers.

Prevention of swarming

Swarming is a natural impulse of the beehive. You may be able to reduce the swarming impulse, but

never entirely eliminate it!! Several factors can assist in minimizing the swarming impulse.

i) Reduce the number of drone cells in the hive. All hives naturally produce drone cells but excessive

numbers are an inducement to swarming. It is prudent to minimize the number of drones in the hive.

This can be achieved by removing frames with large numbers of drone cells from the brood box to

the super (assuming there is a queen excluder in place). Destroying drone cells has minimal impact

as they will simply be rebuilt and reused.

ii) Destroy any swarm queen cells. Examination of any combs in the hive that the queen has access

to approx. every 10/12 days is vital if you are to detect and remove any developing queen cells to

minimize the opportunity for swarming.

iii) Maintain a young vigorous queen. As a young queen exudes more pheromones than an old,

ageing queen, she exerts more control over the bees in the hive and inhibits their swarming

tendencies. If a new, young queen is introduced into the hive, ensure she has adequate laying

room.

iv) Congestion in the hive. This is generally the most common cause of swarming. Ensure that there

is always plenty of room in the brood box for the queen to lay. If necessary, remove frames of brood

from the brood box into the super and those frames in the brood box with empty drawn worker

comb or foundation.

Alternatively, take some frames of brood from a crowded brood box, and place into another hive

needing additional brood. If doing this, ensure that the brood being relocated are free from disease

(AFB, EFB, etc) and mites!! Replace these frames as already described.

Excess combs of honey may be similarly removed, and replaced with fresh foundation or drawn

comb, to create additional space.

v) Artificial Swarming.

This can be used to "trick" the hive into not swarming. There are a variety of methods available (See

Aust. Beekeeping Guide pg 44) to achieve this.

Assuming you have a double hive, one of the simplest methods is as follows:

a) Remove five frames of brood, with bees, from the brood box and place into a spare super.

b) Replace these removed frames with fresh foundation, or drawn comb,

c) Make sure the queen is in the bottom box.

d) Place an excluder on the top of this box.

e) Place a super containing combs of honey, empty combs and some foundation on top of the

excluder.

f) Put a division board with its entrance at the rear or side on top of what is now a twostorey hive.

g) Place the spare super used in a) that contains the frames of brood and bees, above this division

board and introduce a capped queen cell or caged laying queen immediately.
